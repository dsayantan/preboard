Question 1

Q1Practical — Ray Optics (Experiment 1: f of a convex lens, u–v method)[10]

(i) This experiment determines the focal length of a convex lens by no parallax method.

(ii) Determine the approximate focal length \(f\) of the given convex lens by projecting the image of a distant object formed by the lens on a wall or a screen. Record the value of f in cm upto one decimal place.

(iii) Mount the given convex lens on the lens holder. Adjust the heights of object pin O and image pin \(I\) so that their tips lie on the principal axis of the lens.

(iv) Keep the object pin O to the left of the convex lens L, and image pin I to the right of the lens. Keep the object pin at zero cm. mark and the convex lens at 70.0 cm. mark of the optical bench; so that the object distance \(u = OL = 70.0\) cm.

(v) Look at the object pin through the lens, from a distance. You would see an inverted image, \(I'\) of the object pin. Adjust the position of the image pin in such a way that there is no parallax between \(I\) and \(I'\). At no parallax position, note the position of image pin. Measure the image distance \(v = LI\), i.e. distance of the image pin from the lens in cm. correct to one decimal place. Record the value of \(v\) in your answer book and show this reading to the Visiting Examiner.

(vi) Calculate \(x = u - f\) and \(y = \dfrac{100}{v - f}\), both up to one decimal place.

(vii) Now repeat the experiment for \(u\) = 60 cm, 50 cm, 40 cm, 30 cm and 20 cm. Each time, find \(u, v\) and \(y\). Now, tabulate all six sets of values of \(u, v, x\) and \(y\) with their units. Plot a graph of \(y\) vs. \(x\). Draw the line of best fit.

(viii) Determine its slope \(m\) using: \(m = \dfrac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}\)

(ix) Record the value of \(m\) in your answer book, correct up to three significant figures. Hence calculate the focal length of the lens using: \(f = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt m}\) cm.

Answer

Method (brief). Focus a distant window on the wall to get an approximate \(f\). Set the object pin at 0 cm and the lens at 70.0 cm; looking from beyond the image pin, move the image pin until its tip and the tip of the inverted image move together when the eye is moved sideways (no parallax). Read \(v\). Repeat for the other values of \(u\).

The readings below are sample readings (realistic values for typical apparatus, recorded to the least count) — your own readings will differ.

(ii) Approximate focal length (distant object): f = 15.1 cm.

(v)–(vii) Observations (least count of the scale 0.1 cm):

S. No.u (cm)v (cm)x = u − f (cm) y = 100/(v − f) (cm⁻¹)

Graph of \(y\) against \(x\): a straight line through (very nearly) the origin; the best-fit line is drawn with the points evenly on both sides of it.

(viii) Slope from two points P and Q on the line (not plotted points), more than half the line apart: \(m = \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{22.56 - 4.53}{50.0 - 10.0}\)

(ix) \(m = 0.451\) cm\(^{-2}\)   \(f = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{m}} = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{0.451}}\) = 14.9 cm

Why it works: Newton's form of the lens formula, \((u-f)(v-f) = f^2\), gives \(y = \dfrac{100}{v-f} = \dfrac{100}{f^2}(u-f) = \dfrac{100}{f^2}\,x\), so the slope is \(m = 100/f^2\) and \(f = 10/\sqrt m\).

Precautions: tips of the pins and the optical centre on one horizontal line; remove parallax tip to tip, with the eye at least 25 cm from the image pin; apply index correction if the bench scale requires it.

Concepts to recall

  • Lens formula (real-is-positive distances) \(\frac1u+\frac1v=\frac1f\) ⇔ Newton \((u-f)(v-f)=f^2\).
  • No parallax: image and pin move together when the eye moves sideways.
  • Slope from two well-separated points on the line, to 3 s.f.

Q2Practical — Current Electricity (Experiments 8/9: resistance and resistivity of a wire)[8]

A. This experiment determines resistivity of the material of the given wire.

You are provided with one-meter long wire AB attached to a meter scale with terminals at the two ends. You are also provided with a resistance box RB, a voltmeter of range 0-3 V, an ammeter of range 0-1A, a 4V dc power supply 'E', a plug key K, a jockey J and a few connecting wires.

(i) Determine and record the least count of the given voltmeter and ammeter with proper units in the answer booklet.

(ii) Set up a circuit as shown in Figure 2 below. Ensure that all connections are right and tight.

(iii) Take out 1 ohm plug from the resistance box so that R = 1 ohm. Ensure that all other plugs are tightly closed. Place the jockey J at a point C on the wire AB, such that AC = 80 cm. The reading of the voltmeter as well as the ammeter must be within its range. Read and record the readings of the voltmeter and the ammeter, i.e. V and I, with proper units.

(iv) Repeat the experiment to obtain four more sets of reading R, V and I by increasing the resistance R by 1 ohm each time. Ensure that the jockey is always kept at the same position C such that AC = 80 cm in all five sets of readings.

(v) Show any one of the readings in (iv) above to the visiting examiner.

(vi) Determine the value of resistance r using: r = V/I for each set, correct upto three significant figures.

(vii) Now, tabulate all the five sets of values of R, V, I and r with proper units.

(viii) Find \(\mathbf{r_0}\), the mean of all the five values of r and record its value in your answer booklet.

B. (i) Determine and record the least count of the given micrometer screw gauge in cm.
(ii) Using it, calculate the diameter 'd' of the given specimen wire 'X' and record its value in cm in your answer booklet.
(iii) Calculate the resistivity ρ of the material of the wire, using the formula: \(\rho = \dfrac{\pi d^2 r_0}{320}\)

Answer

A (i) Least counts (one division of each meter; the values below are those of the school's meters): voltmeter (0–3 V) 0.05 V; ammeter (0–1 A) 0.02 A.

Method. The voltmeter reads the p.d. \(V\) across AC = 80.0 cm of the wire, the ammeter the current \(I\) through it, so \(r = V/I\) is the resistance of 80.0 cm of wire. Changing \(R\) changes \(I\); the ratio \(V/I\) should stay constant (Ohm's law).

The readings below are sample readings (realistic values for typical apparatus, recorded to the least count) — your own readings will differ.

(viii) \(r_0 = \dfrac{3.08 + 3.11 + 3.12 + 3.10 + 3.07}{5}\) = \(r_0 = 3.10\ \Omega\) (resistance of 80.0 cm of the wire).

B (i) Micrometer: pitch 0.5 mm, 50 circular-scale divisions ⇒ least count \(= 0.5/50 = 0.01\) mm = 0.001 cm (zero error, if any, to be subtracted).

(ii) Diameter at four places, in two perpendicular directions (sample readings): 0.041, 0.040, 0.039, 0.040 cm; mean \(d = 0.040\) cm

(iii) \(\rho = \dfrac{\pi d^2 r_0}{320} = \dfrac{3.142\times(0.040)^2\times3.10}{320}\) = \(\rho = 4.87\times10^{-5}\ \Omega\) cm \(= 4.87\times10^{-7}\ \Omega\) m — of the order expected for constantan.

Where 320 comes from: \(\rho = \dfrac{r_0 A}{l} = \dfrac{r_0\,\pi d^2/4}{80} = \dfrac{\pi d^2 r_0}{320}\) with \(l = AC = 80.0\) cm, so \(d\) in cm gives \(\rho\) in Ω cm.

Precautions: keep the key open between readings so the wire does not heat; press the jockey gently at the same point C; meter readings within range and read without parallax; measure \(d\) at several places in perpendicular directions.

Concepts to recall

  • Ohm's law \(V = Ir\); resistivity \(\rho = rA/l\), \(A = \pi d^2/4\).
  • Least count = value of one smallest division; micrometer LC = pitch / number of circular divisions.
  • Readings recorded to the least count; derived values to the figures asked.
