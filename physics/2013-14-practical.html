Q1Optics — Ray Optics (Practical 1: f of a convex lens, no-parallax method)[10]

This experiment determines the focal length of the given convex lens by no parallax method. You are provided with: (i) an optical bench (ii) a lens holder (iii) a convex lens (iv) two optical pins.

Determine approximate focal length F of the given convex lens by projecting the image of a distant object formed by the lens on a wall or a screen. Record the value of F in cm up to one decimal place. Mount the given convex lens on the lens holder. Adjust the heights of object pin 'O' and image pin 'I' so that their tips lie on the principal axis of the lens. Keep the object pin 'O' to the left of the convex lens L and image pin 'I' to the right of the lens, as shown in Figure 1.

Keep the object pin at zero cm mark and the convex lens at 70 cm mark; so that the object distance u = OL = 70 cm. Look at the object pin through the lens, from a distance. You would see an inverted image (say I') of the object pin. Adjust the position of the image pin I in such a way, that there is no parallax between I' and I. Ensure that it is a 'tip to tip' parallax. At no parallax, note the position of the image pin. Measure the image distance v = LI, i.e. distance of the image pin from the lens in cm correct up to one decimal place. Record this value of v in your answer book.

Calculate \(x = u - F\) and \(y = \dfrac{100}{v-F}\), both up to one decimal place.

Now, repeat the experiment for u = 60 cm, 50 cm, 40 cm, 30 cm and 20 cm. Each time, find v, x and y. Now, tabulate all six sets of values of u, v, x and y with their units. Plot a graph of y vs. x. Draw the line of best fit.

Determine its slope m using the equation: \(m = \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\). Record the value of m in your answer book, correct up to three significant figures.

Calculate f using: \(f = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt m}\) and record its value in your answer book, correct up to one decimal place.

Graph of y against x (origin at (0, 0), uniform scales using most of the sheet):

Slope from two points on the line (not plotted points), far apart: \(m = \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \dfrac{24.67 - 1.73}{56.0 - 4.0} = \dfrac{22.94}{52.0}\) ⇒ \(m = 0.441\ \mathrm{cm^{-2}}\)

Focal length: \(f = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt m} = \dfrac{10}{\sqrt{0.441}} = \dfrac{10}{0.6641}\) ⇒ \(f = 15.1\ \mathrm{cm}\)

Why the method works (not required in the answer): from \(\frac1v+\frac1u=\frac1f\) with real distances positive one gets Newton's form \((u-f)(v-f)=f^2\). With \(F\approx f\), \(y = \dfrac{100}{v-f} = \dfrac{100}{f^2}(u-f) = \dfrac{100}{f^2}\,x\), a straight line through the origin of slope \(m = 100/f^2\), hence \(f = 10/\sqrt m\).

Precautions: tips of O, I and the optical centre of L on one horizontal line; parallax removed tip to tip with the eye at least 25 cm behind I; index correction (bench error) applied if the pins are not at the marked positions.

Concepts to recall

  • Lens formula \(\frac1v+\frac1u=\frac1f\) (real distances positive) ⇔ \((u-f)(v-f)=f^2\).
  • No parallax: image and pin move together when the eye moves sideways.
  • Slope: two far-apart points on the line, covering more than half of it.
Q2Current Electricity (Practical 8: metre bridge, resistance of a wire)[7]

This experiment determines the resistance of a metallic wire. You are provided with: (i) a 100 cm long and uniform wire AB attached to a meter scale on a wooden board. (ii) a 50 cm long and uniform wire R on a wooden frame. (iii) a 10 cm long specimen wire W on a piece of card board. (iv) a Resistance Box. (v) a dc source E (vi) a central zero galvanometer G (vii) a plug key K. (viii) a jockey J. (ix) a few connecting wires. (x) a micrometer Screw Gauge.

Arrange the given apparatus as shown in Figure 2 below. Connect various components as shown in Figure 2. Ensure that all connections are tight.

Close the key K. Introduce a resistance x = 1 Ω in the branch MD by removing a 1 Ω plug from the resistance box. All other plugs must be kept tightly closed. Press the jockey J lightly at different points on the wire AB till at a certain point C, the galvanometer G shows no deflection. Note and record the length \(l\) = AC, correct up to 0.1 cm.

Repeat the experiment for x = 2 Ω, 3 Ω, 4 Ω, 5 Ω and 6 Ω. (x should be in the range of 1–10 Ω). Find the null point each and every time and record the different values of \(l\).

For each value of x, calculate \(y = \dfrac{l}{(100-l)}\) correct up to second decimal place.

Now tabulate all six sets of values of x, \(l\) and y. Plot a graph of y versus x. Draw the line of best fit. Determine its slope S using: \(S = \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\)

Record its value in your answer book, correct up to three significant figures.

Calculate resistance R of the wire using: \(R = \dfrac1S\) and record its value up to one decimal place, with its unit.

Precautions: null point kept near the middle of the wire; jockey pressed gently (not slid); current passed only while taking a reading; plugs of the box tight.

Concepts to recall

  • Balanced metre bridge: \(\frac PQ=\frac{l}{100-l}\).
  • Here P = x (box, left gap), Q = R (wire, right gap) ⇒ \(y = x/R\).
  • Best accuracy when l is near 50 cm.
Q3Current Electricity (Practical 8: specific resistance — diameter by screw gauge)[3]

You are provided with a specimen wire W and a micrometer screw gauge. Determine and record its least count in mm. Now determine the diameter d of the specimen wire, using the screw gauge and record its value in mm.

Calculate K using: \(K = \dfrac{\pi R d^2}{200}\) where R is the resistance of the wire obtained in Question 2.

(Note: R is not the radius of the wire and K is not the specific resistance.)

Answer

The readings below are sample readings generated from plausible apparatus values (rounded to the least count) to show how the answer is set out — in the examination you record your own.

Least count = pitch / number of circular-scale divisions = \(\dfrac{0.5\ \mathrm{mm}}{50}\) ⇒ LC = 0.01 mm (zero error: nil).

Mean diameter d = 0.36 mm

\(K = \dfrac{\pi R d^2}{200} = \dfrac{3.142\times2.5\times(0.36)^2}{200} = \dfrac{3.142\times2.5\times0.1296}{200}\) ⇒ \(K = 5.09\times10^{-3}\) (with d in mm, R in Ω)

What K means: the resistivity of the 50 cm wire is \(\rho = \dfrac{R\,\pi d^2}{4L}\). With d in mm and L = 500 mm, \(\rho = \dfrac{\pi R d^2}{2000}\ \Omega\,\mathrm{mm} = K\times10^{-4}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m \approx 5.1\times10^{-7}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\) (a constantan-like alloy).

Concepts to recall

  • LC = pitch / no. of circular divisions.
  • Take readings in two perpendicular directions at several places; subtract zero error.
  • \(\rho = RA/L = R\pi d^2/4L\).
