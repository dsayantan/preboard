Q1(A)(i)Electrostatics[1]

Three small charged spheres X, Y and Z carrying charges + q, -q and + q respectively are placed equidistant from each other, as shown in the figure. The spheres Y and Z are held in place. Initially, X was also held in place but was otherwise free to move. When X is released, the path followed by it will be towards:

a) A   b) B   c) C   d) D

Answer

b) B

X is at the same distance r from Y and from Z, so the two forces on it have the same magnitude \(F = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q^2}{r^2}\).

Y (−q) attracts X along XY (down and to the left, 60° below the line XA); Z (+q) repels X along ZX produced (up and to the left, 60° above XA). The vertical components (\(F\sin60^\circ\)) cancel and the horizontal ones add: the initial force is \(F_{\text{net}} = 2F\cos60^\circ = F\), parallel to YZ, along XA.

X starts from rest, so it begins to move along XA — but the force does not stay along XA. As soon as X has moved to the left it is nearer to Y and farther from Z: the attraction towards Y (which points downwards) grows, the repulsion from Z (which points upwards) weakens, and a net downward force appears.

So the path starts along XA and curves downwards, towards Y — the curved path B. (Path A, a straight line, would need the force to stay along XA throughout, which it does not.)

Concepts to recall

  • Coulomb's law: like charges repel, unlike attract; \(F \propto q_1q_2/r^2\).
  • Superposition: add the forces as vectors (resolve and add components).
  • A charge released from rest starts off along the net force; its path is straight only if the force keeps that direction.
Q1(A)(ii)Current Electricity[1]

If the ammeter reading in the given circuit is zero, find the value of the resistance R.

a) 50 Ω   b) 100 Ω   c) 150 Ω   d) 200 Ω

Answer

c) 150 Ω

No current flows in the branch A–ammeter–D–4 V cell–C, so there is no drop across the ammeter and the 4 V cell is on open circuit: \(V_A - V_C = 4\) V (the + terminal of the 4 V cell is at D, which is at the potential of A).

The 10 V cell then drives a single current I round the loop A → 100 Ω → C → R → B → A: \(I = \dfrac{10}{100 + R}\).

The p.d. across the 100 Ω resistor must equal 4 V: \(I\times100 = 4 \Rightarrow I = 0.04\) A.

\(100 + R = \dfrac{10}{0.04} = 250\ \Omega\) ⇒ \(R = 150\ \Omega\)

Concepts to recall

  • Zero current through a cell ⇒ p.d. across it = its emf.
  • Kirchhoff's loop law: sum of emfs = sum of IR drops round a loop.
Q1(A)(iii)Current Electricity[1]

Four cells E1, E2, E3 and E4 are connected as shown in the figure. E.M.F of the battery so formed is

a) 6 V   b) 12 V   c) 4V   d) zero

Answer

a) 6 V

Upper branch: E1, E2, E3 in series, all with the + terminal towards the left, so their emfs add: 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 V.

Lower branch: E4 = 6 V, also with + towards the left. Two sources of equal emf (6 V) in parallel, + to +, give a combination of emf 6 V (no current circulates between them when no external current is drawn).

Concepts to recall

  • Cells in series (same sense): emfs add.
  • Equal emfs in parallel: emf of the combination = that emf (only the internal resistance changes).
Q1(A)(iv)Magnetic Effects of Current and Magnetism[1]

Two statements are given below. One is Assertion (A) and the other is Reason (R). Read the statements carefully and choose the option that correctly describes statements A and R.

Assertion (A): The kinetic energy of a charged particle describing a circular path in a uniform magnetic field does NOT remain constant with time.

Reason (R): The velocity of a charged particle moving in a circular path in a uniform magnetic field changes with time.

a) Both assertion and reason are true and reason is the correct explanation for assertion.
b) Both assertion and reason are true and reason is not the correct explanation for assertion.
c) Assertion is true but the reason is false.
d) Assertion is false but the reason is true.

Answer

d) Assertion is false but the reason is true.

The magnetic force \(\vec F = q(\vec v\times\vec B)\) is always perpendicular to \(\vec v\), so it does no work: the speed, and hence the kinetic energy, stays constant — the Assertion is false.

The force continually changes the direction of motion, so the velocity (a vector) does change with time — the Reason is true.

Concepts to recall

  • \(\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B\) ⊥ \(\vec v\) ⇒ work done = 0, speed constant.
  • Radius \(r = mv/qB\).
Q1(A)(v)Magnetic Effects of Current and Magnetism[1]

Lorenz's force is

a) the vector sum of electrostatic and magnetic force acting on a moving charged particle.
b) the sum of electrostatic and magnetic force acting on a moving charged particle.
c) electrostatic force acting only on a charged particle.
d) the magnetic force acts only on a moving charged particle.

Answer

a) the vector sum of electrostatic and magnetic force acting on a moving charged particle.

\(\vec F = q\vec E + q(\vec v\times\vec B)\): the electric and magnetic forces are in general in different directions, so they add as vectors.

Concepts to recall

  • Lorentz force \(\vec F = q(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)\).
Q1(A)(vi)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[1]

An alternating current (a.c.) voltage source at 50 Hz is applied to a series combination of resistor A of resistance ‘P’ and component B of reactance ‘Y’. The phase angle is + π/4. Which of the following is true?

a) B is a capacitor and Y = 2P.   b) B is an inductor and Y = 2P.
c) B is a capacitor and Y = P.   d) B is an inductor and Y = P.

Answer

d) B is an inductor and Y = P.

For a series R–X circuit \(\tan\varphi = \dfrac{X}{R}\); \(\varphi = \pi/4\) ⇒ \(\tan\varphi = 1\) ⇒ \(Y = P\).

The phase angle φ (of the voltage relative to the current) is positive, i.e. the voltage leads the current — the behaviour of an inductive circuit (\(\tan\varphi = (X_L - X_C)/R \gt 0\)). So B is an inductor.

[The phase angle of a circuit is, by the ISC convention, the angle by which the applied voltage leads the current (\(V = V_0\sin\omega t\), \(I = I_0\sin(\omega t - \varphi)\)). Read the other way round — current leading by π/4 — the answer would be (c).]

Concepts to recall

  • \(\tan\varphi = (X_L - X_C)/R\); φ > 0: voltage leads (inductive), φ < 0: current leads (capacitive).
Q1(A)(vii)Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter[1]

ASSERTION: A photon is different from a normal particle.
REASON 1: The energy of the photon in motion is purely kinetic as it has no rest energy.
REASON 2: A photon travels with the speed of light in a vacuum.
REASON 3: The energy of the photon at rest becomes purely potential as its rest mass is zero.

Select the correct option.

a) Assertion is correct. Only reason 3 is correct.
b) Assertion is incorrect. Only Reason 2 is correct.
c) Assertion is correct. Only reasons 1 and 2 are correct.
d) Assertion is incorrect. Only Reasons 1 & 3 are correct.

Answer

c) Assertion is correct. Only reasons 1 and 2 are correct.

A photon has zero rest mass, so it has no rest energy (\(m_0c^2 = 0\)); all its energy \(E = h\nu = pc\) is energy of motion — Reason 1 is correct. It always moves at c in vacuum — Reason 2 is correct. These make it unlike a material particle — the Assertion is correct.

A photon is never at rest, so it cannot have "energy at rest" of any kind — Reason 3 is wrong.

Concepts to recall

  • Photon: rest mass 0, speed c, \(E = h\nu\), \(p = h/\lambda = E/c\).

Q1(B)(i)Optics — Wave Optics[1]

A blue-coloured alternate band of diffraction pattern appears on a screen due to the blue light of wavelength λ passing through a single slit of width ω. If the blue light is replaced with a green light of wavelength 1.5 λ, to what width should you change the slit to get the original pattern back?

Answer

The pattern is fixed by the positions of the minima: \(\omega\sin\theta_n = n\lambda\), i.e. \(\sin\theta_n = n\lambda/\omega\). To keep \(\theta_n\) unchanged with \(\lambda' = 1.5\lambda\), \(\lambda/\omega\) must stay constant.

\(\omega' = \omega\times\dfrac{1.5\lambda}{\lambda}\) ⇒ \(\omega' = 1.5\,\omega\) (the slit must be widened 1.5 times).

Concepts to recall

  • Single slit minima: \(a\sin\theta = n\lambda\).
  • Angular width of central maximum \(2\lambda/a\).
Q1(B)(ii)Optics — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments[1]

How is the refractive index of a prism's material dependent on the angle of deviation and angle of prism in minimum deviation conditions?

Answer

At minimum deviation \(\delta_m\) the ray passes symmetrically (\(r_1 = r_2 = A/2\), \(i = e = (A + \delta_m)/2\)), and

\(\mu = \dfrac{\sin\left(\dfrac{A + \delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\dfrac{A}{2}\right)}\) where A is the angle of the prism and \(\delta_m\) the angle of minimum deviation.

Concepts to recall

  • Prism: \(r_1 + r_2 = A\), \(\delta = i + e - A\).
  • At \(\delta_m\): \(i = e\), \(r_1 = r_2 = A/2\).
Q1(B)(iii)Atoms and Nuclei — Atoms[1]

An electron is revolving around the nucleus of a hydrogen atom. If the radius of the first orbit is \(0.53\times10^{-10}\) m, calculate the de Broglie wavelength of the moving electron in the orbit.

Answer

Bohr's condition \(mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}\) with \(\lambda = \dfrac{h}{mv}\) gives \(2\pi r = n\lambda\): the orbit holds a whole number of de Broglie wavelengths. For n = 1:

\(\lambda = 2\pi r_1 = 2\times3.14\times0.53\times10^{-10}\) ⇒ \(\lambda = 3.33\times10^{-10}\) m

Concepts to recall

  • \(\lambda = h/mv\).
Q1(B)(iv)Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter[1]

Ultra-violet light is incident on a zinc surface and photoelectrons are emitted. The graph shows how the stopping potential \(V_S\) varies with frequency \(f\). How to determine Plank's constant from the graph?

Answer

Einstein's equation: \(eV_S = hf - \phi_0\) ⇒ \(V_S = \left(\dfrac{h}{e}\right)f - \dfrac{\phi_0}{e}\), a straight line of slope \(h/e\).

So find the slope of the line (\(\Delta V_S/\Delta f\)) and multiply by the electronic charge: \(h = e\times\text{slope} = e\,\dfrac{\Delta V_S}{\Delta f}\)

Concepts to recall

  • \(eV_S = hf - \phi_0\); \(\phi_0 = hf_0\).
  • Slope of \(V_S\)–\(f\) graph is \(h/e\) for every metal.

Q1(B)(vi)Electronic Devices[1]

Define depletion region.

Answer

The depletion region is the thin layer on either side of a p-n junction which is depleted of free charge carriers (electrons and holes), containing only the immobile ions — positive donor ions on the n-side and negative acceptor ions on the p-side — formed by diffusion of carriers across the junction and recombination. The electric field of these ions sets up the potential barrier.

Concepts to recall

  • Diffusion of majority carriers → recombination → uncovered immobile ions.
  • Forward bias narrows it; reverse bias widens it.
Q1(B)(vii)Electrostatics[1]

A parallel plate capacitor of capacitance C is charged to a potential V by a battery. Q is the charge stored in the capacitor. The battery is then disconnected and the distance between the plates of the capacitor is increased by a small amount. What changes will occur in its capacitance? Will it increase, decrease or remain the same? Explain.

Answer

The capacitance decreases: \(C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0A}{d}\), so a larger plate separation d gives a smaller C.

(With the battery disconnected the charge Q stays the same, so \(V = Q/C\) increases.)

Concepts to recall

  • \(C = \varepsilon_0A/d\).
  • Battery disconnected ⇒ Q constant; connected ⇒ V constant.

Q2Optics — Wave Optics[2]

Young’s double-slit experiment is first performed in air and then in a medium other than air. It is found that the 8th bright fringe in the medium lies with the 5th dark fringe in the air. Find the refractive index of the medium.

Answer

In a medium of refractive index μ the wavelength becomes \(\lambda/\mu\), so the n-th bright fringe is at \(y_n = \dfrac{n\lambda D}{\mu d}\).

The n-th dark fringe in air is at \(y = \dfrac{(2n-1)\lambda D}{2d}\); for n = 5: \(y = \dfrac{9\lambda D}{2d} = 4.5\,\dfrac{\lambda D}{d}\).

Equating: \(\dfrac{8\lambda D}{\mu d} = \dfrac{4.5\lambda D}{d}\) ⇒ \(\mu = \dfrac{8}{4.5} = \dfrac{16}{9}\)

\(\mu \approx 1.78\)

Concepts to recall

  • Bright: \(y_n = n\lambda D/d\); dark: \(y_n = (2n-1)\lambda D/2d\).
  • In a medium \(\lambda_m = \lambda/\mu\), so fringes shrink by μ.

Q3(b)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[2]

(OR) b) Draw a labelled graph showing the variation of XL and XC with the frequency of the alternating voltage source.

Answer

\(X_L = 2\pi fL \propto f\): a straight line through the origin.

\(X_C = \dfrac{1}{2\pi fC} \propto \dfrac1f\): a rectangular hyperbola, very large at low frequency (infinite for d.c.) and tending to zero at high frequency.

The two curves cross at the resonant frequency \(f_0 = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\), where \(X_L = X_C\).

Concepts to recall

  • \(X_L = \omega L\), \(X_C = 1/\omega C\), \(\omega = 2\pi f\).
  • An inductor blocks high f; a capacitor blocks low f (and d.c.).

Q5(a)Optics — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments[2]

a) A thin convex lens of focal length f is cut into parts and then combined as shown in Fig 1 and Fig 2. Evaluate the focal length of the combination, in:
(i) figure 1
(ii) figure 2

Answer

Cutting a lens along its principal axis into an upper and a lower half does not change the curvature of either surface, so by the lens maker's formula \(\dfrac1f = (\mu - 1)\left(\dfrac1{R_1} - \dfrac1{R_2}\right)\) each half still has focal length f (only the aperture, hence the brightness, is halved).

(i) Fig 1: the two halves are placed one behind the other, in contact — two thin lenses of focal length f in contact: \(\dfrac1F = \dfrac1f + \dfrac1f = \dfrac2f\) ⇒ \(F = f/2\)

(ii) Fig 2: an upper half and a lower half, again in contact: \(\dfrac1F = \dfrac1f + \dfrac1f\) ⇒ \(F = f/2\)

In Fig 2 the two halves are not coaxial — the flat (axis) edge of one is displaced from that of the other. This does not change the focal length of thin lenses in contact: for a ray at height y the deviations \((y - a_1)/f\) and \((y - a_2)/f\) add to \(\left(y - \tfrac{a_1 + a_2}{2}\right)\dfrac{2}{f}\), a single lens of focal length f/2 whose axis lies midway between the two.

Concepts to recall

  • Lens maker: f depends only on μ, R₁, R₂ — not on the aperture.
  • Thin lenses in contact: \(1/F = 1/f_1 + 1/f_2\), \(P = P_1 + P_2\).
  • (Cutting perpendicular to the axis gives two plano-convex lenses of focal length 2f.)
Q5(b)Optics — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments[2]

(OR) b) An object placed at a distance of x from the first focus of a convex lens forms a real image at a distance of y from its second focus. Show that the product xy is equal to the square of the focal length f of the lens.

Answer

Cartesian convention, distances from the optical centre O, light travelling left to right. The object is at \(u = -(f + x)\) and the real image at \(v = +(f + y)\).

Lens formula: \(\dfrac1v - \dfrac1u = \dfrac1f\) ⇒ \(\dfrac{1}{f + y} + \dfrac{1}{f + x} = \dfrac1f\)

\(f(f + x) + f(f + y) = (f + x)(f + y)\)

\(2f^2 + fx + fy = f^2 + fx + fy + xy\)

\(xy = f^2\) (Newton's formula)

Concepts to recall

  • Lens formula \(1/v - 1/u = 1/f\) with u negative for a real object.
  • Measure x from F₁ and y from F₂.
Q6Optics — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments[2]

A light ray entering a right-angled prism undergoes refraction at the face AC as shown in the figure. What is the refractive index of the prism in the figure?

Answer

The ray strikes AB normally, so it goes straight through, parallel to BC.

∠A = 180° − 90° − 45° = 45°. The ray (parallel to BC) meets AC at 45° to the surface, so the angle of incidence on AC (from the normal) is \(i = 45^\circ\).

The emergent ray grazes along AC: angle of refraction \(e = 90^\circ\). So 45° is the critical angle: \(\mu\sin45^\circ = \sin90^\circ\).

\(\mu = \dfrac{1}{\sin45^\circ}\) ⇒ \(\mu = \sqrt2 \approx 1.41\)

Concepts to recall

  • Normal incidence: no bending.
  • Grazing emergence ⇒ angle of incidence = critical angle; \(\mu = 1/\sin C\).

Q8Atoms and Nuclei — Nuclei[2]

State two necessary conditions for nuclear fusion.

Answer

  • Very high temperature (of the order of \(10^{7}\) K): the light nuclei must have enough kinetic energy to overcome their electrostatic (Coulomb) repulsion and come within the short range (~fm) of the nuclear force.
  • Very high pressure / density of the fuel, held (confined) for long enough, so that collisions between nuclei are frequent and the reaction is self-sustaining.

Concepts to recall

  • Fusion: light nuclei combine (e.g. \(4\,{}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}\rightarrow{}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\)), releasing energy because BE/A increases.
  • Thermonuclear: needs ~\(10^7\) K (as in the sun).

Q9Electrostatics[3]

Derive an expression for a combined capacitance of two capacitors C1 and C2 connected in parallel. Draw the diagram necessary for the derivation.

Answer

C1 and C2 are connected between the same two points A and B, across a source of p.d. V.

The p.d. across each capacitor is the same, V. Their charges are \(Q_1 = C_1V\) and \(Q_2 = C_2V\).

The total charge drawn from the source is \(Q = Q_1 + Q_2 = C_1V + C_2V = (C_1 + C_2)V\).

If a single capacitor C stores the same charge Q at the same p.d. V, \(Q = CV\). Hence \(CV = (C_1 + C_2)V\) ⇒ \(C = C_1 + C_2\)

Concepts to recall

  • Parallel: same V, charges add ⇒ \(C = C_1 + C_2\).
  • Series: same Q, p.d.s add ⇒ \(1/C = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2\).
Q10Electrostatics[3]

A student starts working on a problem on Gauss' theorem to calculate the flux and hence the electric field at a given point due to a system of discreet charges. The teacher gives him certain instructions and the student responds to each instruction as per her understanding of the concept. State whether the student's response is correct or incorrect. Give a reason for your answer.

(a) Examiner: Draw a Gaussian surface for the given system of charges.
Student: Draws a symmetric surface passing through the point at which the electric field is to be calculated.

(b) Examiner: Suggest a way to double the flux through the given surface.
Student: Doubles the surface area of the Gaussian surface.

(c) Examiner: Calculate the electric flux of a point charge of 2 μC, placed 5 cm above the centre of a square plate of side 10 cm, through the given plate.
Student: As the charge is not enclosed by the surface, the flux through the surface is zero.

Answer

(a) Correct. A Gaussian surface is an imaginary closed surface around the charges, chosen through the point where E is wanted, and symmetric so that E has the same magnitude over it and \(\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A\) becomes \(E\times\text{area}\).

(b) Incorrect. By Gauss's theorem \(\phi = q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0\): the flux depends only on the charge enclosed, not on the size or area of the surface. To double the flux, the enclosed charge must be doubled.

(c) Incorrect. The plate is an open surface, and field lines from the charge do pass through it. Imagine a cube of side 10 cm with the charge at its centre (5 cm from each face); the plate is one of its six identical faces, so it receives one-sixth of the total flux.

\(\phi = \dfrac{1}{6}\dfrac{q}{\varepsilon_0} = \dfrac{2\times10^{-6}}{6\times8.85\times10^{-12}}\) ⇒ \(\phi \approx 3.77\times10^{4}\) N m\(^2\) C\(^{-1}\)

[CISCE's competency booklet (Q68) prints this question; its key gives (a) correct, (b) incorrect, (c) incorrect with \(\phi = \tfrac16\times2\times10^{-6}/\varepsilon_0 = 3.77\times10^4\) N m² C⁻¹, and notes that a reason is needed for both correct and incorrect responses.]

Concepts to recall

  • Gauss: \(\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A = q_{\text{enc}}/\varepsilon_0\) for a closed surface.
  • Charge at the centre of a cube: each face gets \(q/6\varepsilon_0\).
Q11Current Electricity[3]

For the given electrical circuit, calculate the current through each branch using Kirchhoff's laws

Answer

Label the junctions P (top) and Q (bottom). Branch currents: \(I_1\) from Q round the left loop through the 3 V cell to P; \(I_2\) from P to Q through 2 Ω; \(I_3\) from P through 5 Ω, 3.5 Ω and the 2 V cell to Q.

Junction rule at P: \(I_1 = I_2 + I_3\) … (1)

Loop rule, left mesh (Q → 3 V cell → P → 2 Ω → Q): \(3 = 8.5I_1 + 2I_2\) … (2)

From (1) and (2): \(3 = 8.5I_2 + 8.5I_3 + 2I_2 = 10.5I_2 + 8.5I_3\). From (3): \(I_3 = \dfrac{2 + 2I_2}{9}\). Substituting: \(27 = 94.5I_2 + 17 + 17I_2\) ⇒ \(I_2 = \dfrac{10}{111.5} = 0.0897\) A.

\(I_3 = \dfrac{2 + 0.179}{9} = 0.242\) A; \(I_1 = I_2 + I_3 = 0.332\) A.

Q12Magnetic Effects of Current and Magnetism[3]

Using Ampere’s Circuital Law deduce the expression for the magnetic field at the centre of a circular coil.

Answer

[Ampère's circuital law cannot give the field at the centre of a circular coil: there is no closed path through the centre on which B is constant in magnitude and along the path, so \(\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l\) cannot be evaluated as \(B\times\) length. The ISC syllabus derives this result from the Biot–Savart law, which is what an examiner will accept; it is done below.]

Biot–Savart law: \(dB = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{I\,dl\sin\theta}{r^2}\), where θ is the angle between \(d\vec l\) and the line joining it to the point.

Consider a circular coil of radius r carrying current I, and a small element \(d\vec l\) on it. The line from \(d\vec l\) to the centre O is a radius, which is perpendicular to \(d\vec l\): \(\theta = 90^\circ\).

\(dB = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{I\,dl}{r^2}\), and by the right-hand rule every element gives dB in the same direction — perpendicular to the plane of the coil — so the contributions add directly:

\(B = \displaystyle\oint dB = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi r^2}\oint dl = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{4\pi r^2}\times2\pi r\) ⇒ \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{2r}\); for N turns \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0NI}{2r}\), along the axis.

Concepts to recall

  • Biot–Savart: \(d\vec B = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2}\).
  • Ampère's law \(\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0I\) is useful only with symmetry (long wire, solenoid).

Q15(a)Optics — Wave Optics[3]

(a) With the help of Huygens wave principle show that for the refraction of light \(\mu_1\sin i = \mu_2\sin r\)

Answer

Huygens' principle: every point of a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets which spread out with the speed of light in that medium; the new wavefront is the forward envelope (common tangent) of these wavelets.

A plane wavefront AB in medium 1 (speed \(v_1\)) strikes the surface XY at A. In the time t that the disturbance at B takes to reach C (\(BC = v_1t\)), the wavelet from A spreads into medium 2 to a radius \(AE = v_2t\). The tangent CE from C to this wavelet is the refracted wavefront.

The angle between the incident wavefront and the surface equals the angle of incidence i, and that between the refracted wavefront and the surface equals the angle of refraction r. In right-angled triangles ABC and AEC (right angles at B and E):

\(\sin i = \dfrac{BC}{AC} = \dfrac{v_1t}{AC}\),   \(\sin r = \dfrac{AE}{AC} = \dfrac{v_2t}{AC}\) ⇒ \(\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \dfrac{v_1}{v_2}\)

With \(\mu_1 = c/v_1\) and \(\mu_2 = c/v_2\): \(\dfrac{v_1}{v_2} = \dfrac{\mu_2}{\mu_1}\), so \(\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \dfrac{\mu_2}{\mu_1}\) ⇒ \(\mu_1\sin i = \mu_2\sin r\) (Snell's law). The incident ray, refracted ray and normal lie in one plane — the plane of the figure.

Concepts to recall

  • Huygens: secondary wavelets, envelope = new wavefront.
  • Refractive index \(\mu = c/v\).
  • Wavefront ⊥ ray, so wavefront–surface angle = ray–normal angle.
Q15(b)Optics — Wave Optics[3]

(OR) (b) (i) What do you mean by coherent source of light?
(ii) State one difference between diffraction and interference.
(iii) Draw the intensity distribution graph for the diffraction pattern formed by a single slit.

Answer

(i) Two sources are coherent if they emit light waves of the same frequency (wavelength) with a constant phase difference (zero or fixed) between them, so that the interference pattern stays steady. They are obtained from a single source (e.g. the two slits of Young's experiment illuminated by one slit).

(ii) Any one: interference is the superposition of waves from two coherent sources, diffraction of secondary wavelets from different parts of the same wavefront; in interference all bright fringes have (nearly) the same intensity, in diffraction the intensity of successive maxima falls off rapidly; interference fringes are of equal width, while the central diffraction maximum is twice as wide as the others.

(iii) Central maximum of intensity \(I_0\) at θ = 0, minima at \(\sin\theta = \pm\lambda/a, \pm2\lambda/a, \dots\), secondary maxima near \(\sin\theta = \pm\tfrac{3}{2}\lambda/a, \pm\tfrac52\lambda/a\) of rapidly decreasing intensity (about \(I_0/22\), \(I_0/62\)).

Concepts to recall

  • Single slit: minima \(a\sin\theta = n\lambda\); secondary maxima \(a\sin\theta \approx (n + \tfrac12)\lambda\).
  • Central maximum width \(2\lambda/a\) (angular).

Q18(a)Optics — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments[5]

a) Draw a neat, labelled diagram of image formation by a telescope with the image at least a distance of distinct vision hence stating the formula for angular magnification.

Answer

Astronomical (refracting) telescope, final image at the least distance of distinct vision D. The objective (large focal length \(f_o\), large aperture) forms a real, inverted, diminished image I′ of a distant object at its focus F₀. The eyepiece (short focal length \(f_e\)) is moved so that I′ lies within its focal length, at \(u_e\) from it; it acts as a magnifier and forms the final virtual, magnified image I at distance D from the eye.

Angular magnification \(M = \dfrac{\beta}{\alpha}\), where α is the angle the object subtends at the objective (≈ at the eye) and β the angle the final image subtends at the eye.

If h is the size of I′: \(\alpha \approx \tan\alpha = \dfrac{h}{f_o}\), \(\beta \approx \tan\beta = \dfrac{h}{u_e}\) ⇒ \(M = \dfrac{f_o}{u_e}\).

Eyepiece (lens formula, \(v = -D\), \(u = -u_e\)): \(-\dfrac1D + \dfrac{1}{u_e} = \dfrac{1}{f_e}\) ⇒ \(\dfrac{1}{u_e} = \dfrac{1}{f_e}\left(1 + \dfrac{f_e}{D}\right)\).

\(M = \dfrac{f_o}{f_e}\left(1 + \dfrac{f_e}{D}\right)\) (in magnitude; the final image is inverted). Length of the tube \(L = f_o + u_e\). (For the final image at infinity, \(M = f_o/f_e\) and \(L = f_o + f_e\).)

Concepts to recall

  • Telescope: \(M = \beta/\alpha\).
  • Image at D: \(M = \dfrac{f_o}{f_e}(1 + f_e/D)\); at infinity \(M = f_o/f_e\).
  • Large \(f_o\), small \(f_e\) for high M.

Q19(a)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[5]

a) I) Deduce an expression for
i) induced current
ii) force necessary to pull the conductor and
iii) power delivered by the external source

For a conductor carrying free charge carriers moving in a perpendicular magnetic field. [Draw the necessary diagram for the derivation].

II) What is eddy current? Write one use of it.

Answer

I) A straight conductor PQ of length l slides with constant velocity v on two parallel rails joined through a resistance R, in a uniform magnetic field B perpendicular to the plane of the rails (into the page). The rails and rod have negligible resistance.

If x is the length of the circuit from R to PQ, the flux through it is \(\phi = Blx\), so the induced emf is \(\varepsilon = \left|\dfrac{d\phi}{dt}\right| = Bl\dfrac{dx}{dt} = Blv\). (Equivalently, each free charge in PQ feels \(q\vec v\times\vec B\) along the rod.)

(i) Induced current: \(I = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{R} = \dfrac{Blv}{R}\); by \(q\vec v\times\vec B\) it flows from Q to P in the rod (Lenz's law).

(ii) The current-carrying rod in the field feels \(F_B = BIl = \dfrac{B^2l^2v}{R}\), directed opposite to v. To keep v constant the external force must balance it: \(F = \dfrac{B^2l^2v}{R}\), in the direction of v.

(iii) Power delivered by the external agent: \(P = Fv = \dfrac{B^2l^2v^2}{R}\), which equals \(I^2R\) — the mechanical work done appears as Joule heat in R (energy conservation).

II) Eddy currents are the closed loops of induced current set up inside the body of a bulk conductor when the magnetic flux through it changes (or when it moves in a magnetic field); they oppose the change (Lenz) and heat the conductor.

One use (any one): electromagnetic (eddy current) braking in trains; induction furnace / induction cooking; damping of a moving-coil galvanometer (dead-beat); speedometers; energy meters.

Concepts to recall

  • Motional emf \(\varepsilon = Blv\).
  • Force on current \(F = BIl\); Lenz: it opposes the motion.
  • \(P = Fv = I^2R\).
Q19(b)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[5]

(OR) b) I) An alternating emf is applied to a series combination of a resistor of resistance R, a capacitor of capacitance C and an inductor of inductance L. Derive the phase relationship between current and emf. Find the impedance of the circuit. Draw the necessary phasor diagram.

II) In a galvanometer there is a deflection of 10 divisions per mA. The internal resistance of the galvanometer is 60 Ω. If a shunt of 2.5 Ω is connected to the galvanometer and there are 50 divisions in all on the scale of the galvanometer, what maximum current can this galvanometer read?

Answer

I) An emf \(V = V_0\sin\omega t\) is applied to R, L and C in series.

The same current \(I = I_0\sin(\omega t - \varphi)\) flows through all three. Taking I as the reference phasor: \(V_R = I_0R\) is in phase with I; \(V_L = I_0X_L\) leads I by 90°; \(V_C = I_0X_C\) lags I by 90° (\(X_L = \omega L\), \(X_C = 1/\omega C\)).

\(V_L\) and \(V_C\) are opposite, so their resultant is \(V_L - V_C\) (taking \(V_L \gt V_C\)), at 90° to \(V_R\). The applied emf is the phasor sum:

\(V_0^2 = V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2 = I_0^2\left[R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2\right]\)

Impedance: \(Z = \dfrac{V_0}{I_0} = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2} = \sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}\)

Phase: \(\tan\varphi = \dfrac{V_L - V_C}{V_R} = \dfrac{X_L - X_C}{R}\). If \(X_L \gt X_C\) the emf leads the current by φ; if \(X_L \lt X_C\) the current leads; if \(X_L = X_C\) they are in phase (resonance, Z = R).

II) Full-scale current of the galvanometer: \(I_g = \dfrac{50\ \text{div}}{10\ \text{div/mA}} = 5\) mA.

With the shunt, \(I_gG = (I - I_g)S\) ⇒ \(I = I_g\left(1 + \dfrac GS\right) = 5\times10^{-3}\left(1 + \dfrac{60}{2.5}\right) = 5\times10^{-3}\times25\)

\(I_{\max} = 0.125\) A \(= 125\) mA

Concepts to recall

  • R: V in phase with I; L: V leads by 90°; C: V lags by 90°.
  • \(Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}\), \(\tan\varphi = (X_L - X_C)/R\).
  • Shunt: \(I_gG = (I - I_g)S\).
Q20Electronic Devices[5]

(i) With the help of a suitable circuit diagram (block diagram), sketch the characteristic curves of a junction diode.
(ii) With the help of a circuit diagram, explain full-wave rectification using a junction diode. Draw the waveforms of input and output voltages.

Answer

(i) The p-n junction diode is connected through a milliammeter to a battery via a rheostat used as a potential divider, with a voltmeter across the diode. The p-side is joined to the + side (forward bias). For reverse bias the battery connections are reversed and a microammeter replaces the milliammeter. The voltage is varied in steps and the current noted.

Forward bias: the current is negligible until the voltage exceeds the barrier potential (the knee or threshold voltage, ≈ 0.7 V for Si, 0.3 V for Ge); beyond it the current rises steeply (non-linearly) — a few mA.

Reverse bias: a very small, almost constant current (μA) due to minority carriers, until at the breakdown voltage the reverse current rises suddenly.

(ii) Full-wave (centre-tap) rectifier. The a.c. input is applied to the primary of a transformer whose secondary is centre-tapped at T. The ends S₁ and S₂ feed two diodes D₁ and D₂, whose cathodes are joined at P; the load \(R_L\) is connected between P and T.

During the half-cycle when S₁ is positive with respect to T, D₁ is forward biased and conducts while D₂ is reverse biased; current flows S₁ → D₁ → P → \(R_L\) → T.

In the next half-cycle S₂ is positive; D₂ conducts and D₁ is off; current flows S₂ → D₂ → P → \(R_L\) → T — in the same direction through the load.

So both halves of every input cycle give output: a unidirectional, pulsating d.c. voltage across \(R_L\) (frequency twice the input frequency), which a capacitor filter can smooth.

Concepts to recall

  • Forward bias: p to +; current after the knee voltage.
  • Reverse bias: tiny minority-carrier current; breakdown at high reverse voltage.
  • Full-wave: two diodes conduct alternately; load current in one direction.
