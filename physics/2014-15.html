Q1(A)(i)Electrostatics[1]

Two point charges \(17.7\ \mu C\) and \(-17.7\ \mu C\), separated by a small distance, are kept inside a large hollow metallic sphere of radius 15 cm. Electric flux emanating through the sphere is:

(a) \(2\times10^{6}\) V m   (b) \(-2\times10^{6}\) V m   (c) Zero   (d) \(4\times10^{6}\) V m

Answer

(c) Zero.

By Gauss's theorem \(\phi = q_{\text{enclosed}}/\varepsilon_0\), and the net charge enclosed is \(17.7 - 17.7 = 0\ \mu\)C, so the flux is zero (the radius is irrelevant).

Concepts to recall

  • Gauss: \(\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec A = q_{\rm enc}/\varepsilon_0\).
  • Flux depends only on the net enclosed charge.

Q1(B)(i)Electrostatics[1]

How long will an electron take to fall through a distance of 2 m in a uniform electric field of 250 N/C?

Answer

\(a = \dfrac{eE}{m} = \dfrac{1.6\times10^{-19}\times250}{9\times10^{-31}} = 4.44\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) (gravity is negligible).

Starting from rest: \(s = \tfrac12at^2\Rightarrow t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2s}{a}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\times2}{4.44\times10^{13}}}\)

\(t = 3.0\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm s\)

Concepts to recall

  • \(F = qE\), \(a = qE/m\).
  • From rest: \(s=\frac12at^2\).
Q1(B)(ii)Electrostatics[1]

Five point charges, each of value +q, are placed at five vertices of a regular hexagon of side L m. What is the magnitude of the force on a point charge −q placed at the center of the hexagon?

Answer

With +q at all six vertices the forces on the centre charge would cancel by symmetry. So the resultant of the five equals minus the force the missing sixth charge would exert. The centre is a distance L from every vertex (regular hexagon), so

\(F = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{q^2}{L^2}\), directed from the centre towards the vertex opposite the empty one.

Concepts to recall

  • Superposition; symmetric arrangements cancel.
  • Regular hexagon: centre-to-vertex distance = side.

Q1(B)(xii)Electromagnetic Waves[1]

What is the main difference between Huygen's wave theory of light and Maxwell's electromagnetic theory?

Answer

Huygens treated light as a mechanical wave needing a material medium — a hypothetical all-pervading "ether".

Maxwell showed light is an electromagnetic wave: mutually perpendicular oscillating \(\vec E\) and \(\vec B\), transverse to the direction of travel, needing no medium and travelling in vacuum at \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\).

Concepts to recall

  • EM wave: \(\vec E\perp\vec B\perp\) direction of propagation; \(c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}\).
  • Huygens' principle still used for wavefront construction.

Q1(B)(xv)Atoms and Nuclei — Nuclei[1]

What is the essential difference between the working of a nuclear reactor and that of a fission bomb?

Answer

Reactor: controlled chain reaction — control rods absorb excess neutrons so that the multiplication factor k = 1 and energy is released at a steady rate.

Bomb: uncontrolled chain reaction (k > 1) — the number of fissions grows in each generation and a huge energy is released explosively in a very short time.

Concepts to recall

  • k = 1 critical (reactor); k > 1 supercritical (bomb).

Q2(a)Electrostatics[2]

Obtain an expression of the work done in rotating the dipole through an angle θ from its equilibrium position.

Answer

In a uniform field \(\vec E\) a dipole \(\vec p\) at angle θ feels a torque \(\tau = pE\sin\theta\) (the couple \(qE\times2l\sin\theta\)), tending to restore it to θ = 0.

Work done against this torque in turning it through dθ: \(dW = \tau\,d\theta = pE\sin\theta\,d\theta\).

\[W = \int_0^{\theta} pE\sin\theta\,d\theta = pE\big[-\cos\theta\big]_0^{\theta}\]

\(W = pE(1-\cos\theta)\) (stored as potential energy; e.g. \(W = 2pE\) for θ = 180°).

Concepts to recall

  • \(\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E\).
  • \(U = -\vec p\cdot\vec E\); \(W = U(\theta)-U(0)\).

Q3(a)Electrostatics[2]

Three charges q, −2q, q are arranged on the vertices of an equilateral triangle of side a. What is the equivalent dipole moment?

Answer

Split −2q into two charges −q. The system is then two dipoles, each of moment \(p_1 = p_2 = qa\), directed from the −2q vertex to each +q vertex, with 60° between them.

\(p = \sqrt{p_1^2+p_2^2+2p_1p_2\cos60^\circ} = \sqrt{3}\,qa\)

\(p = \sqrt3\,qa\), along the bisector from the −2q vertex towards the midpoint of the opposite side.

Concepts to recall

  • Dipole moment points from −q to +q, magnitude q × separation.
  • Dipole moments add as vectors.
Q3(b)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[3]

Obtain an expression for self-induction of a solenoid having N-number of turns.

Answer

Long air-cored solenoid: N turns, length l, cross-section A, current I. Field inside (Ampère's law): \(B = \mu_0 nI = \dfrac{\mu_0NI}{l}\).

By definition \(\phi = LI\), so \(L = \dfrac{\mu_0N^2A}{l}\) (\(= \mu_0n^2Al\); with a core of relative permeability \(\mu_r\), \(L = \mu_0\mu_rN^2A/l\)).

Q4(b)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[3]

Q5(a)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[2]

What is eddy current? How it is disadvantageous to us?

Answer

Eddy currents are the closed loops of current induced in the body of a bulk conductor when the magnetic flux through it changes (it moves in a field or the field varies). By Lenz's law they oppose the change that produces them.

Disadvantages: they heat the cores of transformers, motors and generators, wasting electrical energy (I²R loss) and lowering efficiency, and may damage insulation. They are reduced by using laminated cores (thin insulated sheets) or slotted/ferrite cores.

Concepts to recall

  • Faraday + Lenz in a bulk conductor ⇒ eddy currents.
  • Laminations raise the resistance of the eddy paths.
Q5(b)Magnetic Effects of Current and Magnetism[3]

A solenoid of length 1.5 m and radius 2 cm has 5 layers of windings, each having 150 turns. Find magnetic flux coming out through one of its ends of cross-section. The current through the solenoid is 10 A.

Answer

Total turns \(N = 5\times150 = 750\); \(n = N/l = 750/1.5 = 500\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\).

At an end of a long solenoid the field is half that inside: \(B_{\text{end}} = \tfrac12\mu_0nI = \tfrac12\times4\times3.14\times10^{-7}\times500\times10 = 3.14\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm T\).

\(A = \pi r^2 = 3.14\times(0.02)^2 = 1.256\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2}\)

\(\phi = BA = 3.14\times10^{-3}\times1.256\times10^{-3}\) ⇒ \(\phi \approx 3.9\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{Wb}\)

Why exactly half at the end face. Place a second identical solenoid against this end: the pair is one long solenoid with field \(\mu_0nI\) at the joining plane. By symmetry each half contributes equally to the axial component there, so at the end face of one solenoid \(B_{\text{axial}} = \tfrac12\mu_0nI\) over the whole cross-section. Only half the flux inside reaches the end face; the rest leaks out through the sides near the end.

The question says "coming out through one of its ends", so the end-face reading is the one intended. Using the field deep inside (\(\mu_0nI\)) gives \(7.9\times10^{-6}\) Wb, which is the flux through a cross-section near the middle, not through the end.

Concepts to recall

  • Long solenoid: \(B=\mu_0nI\) inside, \(\frac12\mu_0nI\) at the ends.
  • \(\phi = BA\) for uniform B ⟂ A.

Q8(a)Optics — Ray Optics[3]

Show that the deviation produced by a thin glass prism placed in air is reduced to one-fourth when the prism is immersed in water. Given: \({}_a\mu_g = 3/2\) and \({}_a\mu_w = 4/3\)

Answer

Thin prism of angle A: \(\delta = (\mu-1)A\), μ being the index of the prism relative to the surrounding medium.

In air: \(\delta_a = \left(\tfrac32-1\right)A = \dfrac A2\).

In water: \({}_w\mu_g = \dfrac{{}_a\mu_g}{{}_a\mu_w} = \dfrac{3/2}{4/3} = \dfrac98\), so \(\delta_w = \left(\tfrac98-1\right)A = \dfrac A8\).

\(\dfrac{\delta_w}{\delta_a} = \dfrac{A/8}{A/2} = \dfrac14\) — reduced to one-fourth.

Concepts to recall

  • Thin prism \(\delta=(\mu-1)A\).
  • Relative index \({}_1\mu_2 = \mu_2/\mu_1\).
Q8(b)Optics — Wave Optics; Electromagnetic Waves[2]

State any one condition for sustained interference of light.

State one dissimilarity between the two electromagnetic waves: X-rays and Microwaves

Answer

Sustained interference: the two sources must be coherent — emit waves of the same frequency with a constant phase difference (in practice derived from a single source). (Also acceptable: nearly equal amplitudes; narrow sources close together.)

X-rays vs microwaves: wavelength — X-rays \(\sim10^{-10}\) m (very short, highly penetrating, ionising), microwaves \(\sim10^{-3}\)–\(10^{-1}\) m. (Or production: X-rays from fast electrons striking a metal target; microwaves from klystrons/magnetrons.)

Concepts to recall

  • Coherent = constant phase difference.
  • EM spectrum order: radio, micro, IR, visible, UV, X, γ (decreasing λ).
Q9(a)Optics — Ray Optics[3]

Obtain an expression for refraction at a single convex spherical surface, i.e. the relation between \(\mu_1\) (rarer medium), \(\mu_2\) (denser medium), the object distance u, image distance v and radius of curvature R.

Answer

A convex surface (pole P, centre C) separates a rarer medium \(\mu_1\) (object side) from a denser medium \(\mu_2\). A ray from the point object O meets the surface at A (height h) and after refraction meets the axis at the real image I. CA (produced to N) is the normal. Let α, β, γ be the angles that AO, AI, AC make with the axis.

From the triangles: exterior angle of △OAC: \(i = \alpha + \gamma\); of △ACI: \(\gamma = r + \beta\Rightarrow r = \gamma-\beta\).

Snell's law for paraxial rays (small angles): \(\mu_1 i = \mu_2 r \Rightarrow \mu_1(\alpha+\gamma) = \mu_2(\gamma-\beta)\).

For small angles \(\alpha\approx\dfrac{h}{PO},\ \beta\approx\dfrac{h}{PI},\ \gamma\approx\dfrac{h}{PC}\) (A is very close to P):

\[\frac{\mu_1}{PO}+\frac{\mu_2}{PI} = \frac{\mu_2-\mu_1}{PC}\]

Cartesian signs (distances from P, positive along the incident light): \(PO = -u,\ PI = +v,\ PC = +R\):

\(\dfrac{\mu_2}{v} - \dfrac{\mu_1}{u} = \dfrac{\mu_2-\mu_1}{R}\)

Concepts to recall

  • Exterior angle of a triangle = sum of interior opposite angles.
  • Paraxial: \(\sin\theta\approx\tan\theta\approx\theta\).
  • Snell \(\mu_1\sin i = \mu_2\sin r\).

  • Object between F and 2F ⇒ magnified real image; inside F ⇒ virtual.
Q10(a)Optics — Ray Optics[3]

An object is placed at a distance of 20 cm to the left of a convex lens of focal length 10 cm. A concave mirror of focal length 5 cm is placed at a distance of 30 cm to the right of the lens. Find the position and nature of the final image. Draw the ray-diagram and locate the position of the final image.

Answer

Lens (u = −20, f = +10): \(\dfrac1v = \dfrac1{10}-\dfrac1{20}\Rightarrow v = +20\) cm; \(I_1\) is real, inverted, same size (m = −1), 20 cm right of the lens, i.e. 10 cm in front of the mirror.

Mirror (u = −10, f = −5): \(\dfrac1v = \dfrac1f-\dfrac1u = -\dfrac15+\dfrac1{10}\Rightarrow v = -10\) cm. \(I_1\) is at the mirror's centre of curvature, so \(I_2\) is formed at the same place, real, inverted with respect to \(I_1\) (m = −1).

Lens again (light now travels right to left; \(I_2\) is 20 cm from the lens = 2f): the image \(I_3\) is formed 20 cm on the other side, i.e. at the original object position, with m = −1.

Final image: 20 cm to the left of the lens (at the object itself), real, inverted, same size (overall \(m = (-1)(-1)(-1) = -1\)). If only the reflection is counted, the image formed by the mirror is 10 cm in front of it (20 cm right of the lens), real and erect relative to the object.

Concepts to recall

  • Image of one element is the object for the next.
  • Object at C of a concave mirror ⇒ image at C, inverted, same size.
  • Object at 2F of a lens ⇒ image at 2F.
Q11(a)(ii)Optics — Ray Optics[1]

State one of the advantages of reflecting telescope over refracting type telescope.

Answer

A reflecting telescope uses a (parabolic) concave mirror as objective, so it has no chromatic aberration (and no spherical aberration). Also: a large mirror is easier and cheaper to make and support than a large lens, giving greater light-gathering and resolving power. (Any one.)

Concepts to recall

  • Mirrors reflect all colours alike — no dispersion.
  • Resolving power ∝ aperture.
Q11(b)Optics — Ray Optics[2]

A compound microscope consists of an objective of focal length 2 cm and an eye piece of focal length 5 cm. When an object is kept 2.4 cm from the objective, final image formed is virtual and is at 25 cm from the eye piece. Determine magnifying power of this compound microscope in this set up i.e. in normal use.

Answer

Objective: \(\dfrac1{v_o} = \dfrac12-\dfrac1{2.4} = \dfrac1{12}\Rightarrow v_o = 12\) cm; \(m_o = \dfrac{v_o}{u_o} = \dfrac{12}{2.4} = 5\).

Eyepiece with final image at D = 25 cm: \(m_e = 1+\dfrac{D}{f_e} = 1+\dfrac{25}{5} = 6\).

\(M = m_o\times m_e = 5\times6\) ⇒ \(M = 30\) (magnitude; the final image is inverted).

Concepts to recall

  • Compound microscope (image at D): \(M = \frac{v_o}{u_o}\left(1+\frac{D}{f_e}\right)\).

Q12(a)Atoms and Nuclei — Atoms[3]

Obtain an expression for the total energy of an electron in the Bohr model of Hydrogen atom. Start from equating the electrostatic force with the centripetal force required and then use the quantization condition for angular momentum.

Answer

Electron (mass m, charge −e) in a circular orbit of radius r about the proton:

\[\frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{e^2}{r^2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad mv^2 = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\qquad(1)\]

Quantisation: \(mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}\Rightarrow v = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi mr}\). Substituting in (1): \(r_n = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0n^2h^2}{\pi me^2}\).

Kinetic energy \(K = \tfrac12mv^2 = \dfrac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\); potential energy \(U = -\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\).

Total \(E = K + U = -\dfrac{e^2}{8\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\); putting \(r_n\):

\(E_n = -\dfrac{me^4}{8\varepsilon_0^2n^2h^2} = -\dfrac{13.6}{n^2}\ \mathrm{eV}\) (negative: the electron is bound; K = −E, U = 2E).

Concepts to recall

  • Coulomb force = centripetal force.
  • \(mvr = nh/2\pi\).
  • \(E = -K = U/2\).
Q13(b)Atoms and Nuclei — Nuclei[2]

Calculate the mass defect of Helium [\({}^4\mathrm{He}_2\)] nucleus and ii) its binding energy in MeV. Mass of proton = 1.007276 u, Mass of neutron = 1.008665 u, mass of \({}^4\mathrm{He}_2\) = 4.001506 u

Answer

\(\Delta m = 2m_p + 2m_n - M = 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) - 4.001506 = 4.031882 - 4.001506\)

(i) \(\Delta m = 0.030376\ \mathrm u\)

(ii) \(BE = \Delta m\times931\ \mathrm{MeV} = 0.030376\times931\) ⇒ \(BE \approx 28.3\ \mathrm{MeV}\) (≈ 7.07 MeV per nucleon)

Concepts to recall

  • \(\Delta m = Zm_p + (A-Z)m_n - M_{\rm nucleus}\).
  • 1 u ≡ 931 MeV.
Q14(a)Electronic Devices[3]

Draw the circuit diagram of a full-wave rectifier using two junction diodes. Label the diagram showing the direction of current through the circuit.

Answer

The a.c. input is applied to the primary of a centre-tapped transformer. The ends S₁, S₂ of the secondary feed diodes D₁, D₂, whose cathodes are joined at X; the load \(R_L\) is connected between X and the centre tap T.

During the half-cycle when S₁ is positive with respect to T, D₁ is forward biased and conducts (D₂ reverse biased): current S₁ → D₁ → X → \(R_L\) → T.

In the next half-cycle S₂ is positive: D₂ conducts and D₁ is off: current S₂ → D₂ → X → \(R_L\) → T.

So the current through the load is always from X to T — a unidirectional (pulsating d.c.) output during both halves of every cycle.

Concepts to recall

  • Diode conducts only when forward biased (p at higher potential).
  • Full-wave: output frequency = twice the input frequency.
