Q1(A)(i)Magnetic Effects of Current and Magnetism[1]

When a charged particle is projected perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field, it describes a circular path in which:

(a) Its speed remains constant   (b) Its velocity remains constant   (c) Its momentum remains constant   (d) Its kinetic energy increases.

Answer

(a) Its speed remains constant.

The magnetic force \(\vec F = q\,\vec v\times\vec B\) is always perpendicular to \(\vec v\), so it does no work: kinetic energy and hence speed stay constant. The direction of \(\vec v\) (and of \(\vec p\)) keeps changing, so velocity and momentum are not constant.

Concepts to recall

  • \(\vec F=q\vec v\times\vec B\perp\vec v\) ⇒ work done = 0.
  • Radius \(r = mv/qB\).
Q1(A)(ii)Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter[1]

The dimensions of Planck's constant are:

(a) \(\mathrm{ML^2T^{-3}}\)   (b) \(\mathrm{ML^2T^{-2}}\)   (c) \(\mathrm{ML^2T^{-1}}\)   (d) \(\mathrm{MLT^{-3}}\)

Answer

(c) \(\mathrm{ML^2T^{-1}}\)

\(E = h\nu \Rightarrow [h] = [E]/[\nu] = \mathrm{ML^2T^{-2}}/\mathrm{T^{-1}} = \mathrm{ML^2T^{-1}}\) (same as angular momentum).

Concepts to recall

  • \(E=h\nu\); \([E]=\mathrm{ML^2T^{-2}}\).
  • h has the dimensions of angular momentum (J s).
Q1(A)(iii)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[1]

The self inductance of a straight conductor is:

(a) 0   (b) Infinite   (c) Very large   (d) Very small

Answer

(d) Very small.

A straight wire has no turns, so the magnetic flux it links with itself per unit current is very small; its self-inductance is tiny (of the order of \(10^{-6}\) H per metre) but not exactly zero. Coiling the wire into many turns is what makes \(L\) large (\(L = \mu_0 N^2A/l\)).

Some question banks give (a) 0 for this item, treating a straight wire as linking no flux. That is an idealisation: a current in a straight wire does set up circular field lines round it, so its self-inductance is small but not zero. (d) is the physically correct choice.

Concepts to recall

  • \(\phi = LI\); \(L\) depends on geometry (turns, area, length) and core.
  • Solenoid: \(L=\mu_0N^2A/l\).
Q1(A)(iv)Atoms and Nuclei — Atoms[1]

In which state a substance emits a line spectra?

(a) Molecular State   (b) Atomic State   (c) Solid State   (d) Liquid state

Answer

(b) Atomic state.

Isolated atoms (e.g. a gas at low pressure) have discrete energy levels, so the photons emitted in transitions have definite wavelengths — a line spectrum. Molecules give band spectra; solids and liquids give continuous spectra.

Concepts to recall

  • Line spectrum ← discrete atomic energy levels, \(h\nu = E_i - E_f\).
  • Molecules → bands; hot solids → continuous.
Q1(A)(v)Current Electricity[1]

A wire of resistance R is cut into ten equal parts which are then joined in parallel. The new resistance is:

(a) 0.01R   (b) 0.1R   (c) 10R   (d) 100R

Answer

(a) 0.01R

Each piece has resistance \(R/10\). Ten equal resistances in parallel: \(R_p = \dfrac{R/10}{10} = \dfrac{R}{100} = 0.01R\).

Concepts to recall

  • \(R\propto l\) for a uniform wire.
  • n equal resistors r in parallel: \(r/n\).

Q1(B)(ii)Magnetic Effects of Current and Magnetism[1]

Write an expression of magnetic flux density 'B' at a point in end-on position or an axial position of a magnetic dipole. (Derivation not required.)

Answer

At distance \(r\) from the centre of a short magnetic dipole of moment \(m\), on its axis (\(r \gg l\)):

\(B_{\text{axial}} = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\dfrac{2m}{r^3}\), directed along \(\vec m\).

(For a magnet of length \(2l\): \(B = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{2mr}{(r^2-l^2)^2}\).)

Concepts to recall

  • Axial: \(\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2m}{r^3}\); equatorial: \(\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{m}{r^3}\) (opposite to \(\vec m\)).
  • Same form as the electric dipole with \(\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\to\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\).
Q1(B)(iii)Magnetic Effects of Current and Magnetism[1]

In a moving coil galvanometer, what is meant by a radial magnetic field?

Answer

A field whose lines are along the radii of the cylindrical soft-iron core, produced by concave (cylindrical) pole pieces with the core at their centre. The plane of the coil therefore always lies along the field in every position, so the angle between \(\vec m\) and \(\vec B\) is always 90° and the deflecting torque is \(\tau = NIAB\) (maximum, independent of deflection) — giving \(I\propto\theta\), a linear scale.

Concepts to recall

  • \(\tau = NIAB\sin\phi\), φ between normal and B.
  • Radial field ⇒ φ = 90° always ⇒ \(NIAB = k\theta\).

Q1(B)(vi)Atoms and Nuclei — Atoms[1]

What conclusion was drawn by Rutherford based on Geiger-Marsden's experiment on scattering of alpha particles?

Answer

Most α-particles passed undeflected, so most of the atom is empty space; a very few were deflected through large angles (some > 90°), so the entire positive charge and almost all the mass of the atom is concentrated in a tiny central nucleus (radius \(\sim10^{-15}\) m, about \(10^{-5}\) of the atomic size), with the electrons revolving around it.

Concepts to recall

  • Large-angle scattering needs a massive, tiny, positive centre.
  • Distance of closest approach: \(r_0 = \frac{2Ze^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 K}\).
Q1(B)(vii)Atoms and Nuclei — Nuclei[1]

Write a balanced nuclear reaction showing emission of \(\beta^-\) particle by \(^{234}\mathrm{Th}_{90}\). (Symbol of daughter nucleus formed in the process is Pa)

Answer

In β⁻ emission a neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton, an electron (the β⁻ particle) and an antineutrino: \(n \rightarrow p + e^- + \bar\nu\). So the mass number A is unchanged and the atomic number Z rises by 1.

\({}^{234}_{\ 90}\mathrm{Th} \;\rightarrow\; {}^{234}_{\ 91}\mathrm{Pa} + {}^{\ \ 0}_{-1}e + \bar\nu\)

Check: A: 234 = 234 + 0; Z: 90 = 91 + (−1). Both are conserved, so the equation is balanced.

ISC 2027 dropped radioactivity as a topic (no decay law, half-life or activity). Balancing a nuclear reaction equation by conserving A and Z is still examinable under "Nuclear reactions" in the Nuclei unit, which is why this item is kept.

Concepts to recall

  • In any nuclear reaction the total mass number A and the total charge (atomic number) Z are conserved.
  • Electron: \({}^{\ \ 0}_{-1}e\); α: \({}^4_2\mathrm{He}\); neutron: \({}^1_0n\); proton: \({}^1_1p\).
  • β⁻: A same, Z + 1.
Q1(B)(viii)Atoms and Nuclei — Nuclei[1]

What is the essential difference between the working of a nuclear reactor and that of a fission bomb?

Answer

Both use a fission chain reaction. In a reactor the chain reaction is controlled: control rods (Cd/B) absorb surplus neutrons so that on average exactly one neutron from each fission causes a further fission (multiplication factor \(k = 1\)); energy is released steadily.

In a fission bomb the chain reaction is uncontrolled (\(k > 1\)): the number of fissions multiplies in each generation and an enormous energy is released in a fraction of a second — an explosion.

Concepts to recall

  • Multiplication factor k: = 1 critical (reactor), > 1 supercritical (bomb).
  • Moderator slows neutrons; control rods absorb them.
Q1(B)(ix)Electronic Devices[1]

State one important use of Zener diode.

Answer

As a voltage regulator: operated in reverse breakdown, the voltage across a Zener diode stays constant (= \(V_Z\)) over a wide range of current, so the load connected across it gets a steady d.c. voltage even when the input voltage or the load current varies.

Concepts to recall

  • Zener: heavily doped, used in reverse bias at breakdown.
  • Series resistor drops the excess input voltage.
Q1(B)(x)Optics — Wave Optics[1]

In a Young's double slit experiment, the angular width of a fringe formed on a distant screen is 0.1°. The wavelength of light used is 6000 Å. Find the spacing between the slits.

Answer

Angular fringe width \(\theta = \beta/D = \lambda/d\) ⇒ \(d = \lambda/\theta\).

\(\theta = 0.1° = 0.1\times\dfrac{\pi}{180} = 1.745\times10^{-3}\) rad; \(\lambda = 6000\) Å \(= 6\times10^{-7}\) m.

\(d = \dfrac{6\times10^{-7}}{1.745\times10^{-3}}\) ⇒ \(d \approx 3.44\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm m = 0.344\ \mathrm{mm}\)

Concepts to recall

  • Fringe width \(\beta = \lambda D/d\); angular width \(\lambda/d\).
  • Convert degrees to radians.
Q1(B)(xii)Optics — Ray Optics[1]

On what factors does the angular dispersion of a prism depend?

Answer

For a thin prism, angular dispersion \(\delta_V-\delta_R = (\mu_V-\mu_R)A\). It depends on

(1) the angle of the prism A, and (2) the material of the prism, through the difference \(\mu_V-\mu_R\) of its refractive indices for the extreme colours (i.e. on the wavelengths considered).

Concepts to recall

  • Thin prism: \(\delta = (\mu-1)A\).
  • Dispersive power \(\omega = \frac{\mu_V-\mu_R}{\mu_Y-1}\) — independent of A.
Q1(B)(xiv)Optics — Ray Optics[1]

In a compound microscope consisting of two convex lenses of focal lengths 1.5 cm and 10 cm, the object is 2 cm from the objective and the final image is formed at the least distance of distinct vision (D = 25 cm). Find the distance of separation of the two lenses.

Answer

Objective (\(f_o = 1.5\) cm, \(u = -2\) cm): \(\dfrac1v-\dfrac1u=\dfrac1f\Rightarrow\dfrac1{v_o}=\dfrac1{1.5}-\dfrac12=\dfrac16\Rightarrow v_o = 6\) cm.

Eyepiece (\(f_e=10\) cm, image at \(v_e=-25\) cm): \(\dfrac1{u_e}=\dfrac1{v_e}-\dfrac1{f_e}=-\dfrac1{25}-\dfrac1{10}=-\dfrac{7}{50}\Rightarrow u_e=-7.14\) cm.

Separation \(L = v_o + |u_e| = 6 + 7.14\) ⇒ \(L \approx 13.1\ \mathrm{cm}\)

Concepts to recall

  • Lens formula \(\frac1v-\frac1u=\frac1f\) (Cartesian signs).
  • Tube length = image distance of objective + object distance of eyepiece.
Q1(B)(xv)Optics — Ray Optics[1]

The size of the moon is much smaller than stars, still it appears bigger. Why?

Answer

The apparent size of an object depends on the visual angle it subtends at the eye, not on its actual size. The moon is very near (\(\approx3.8\times10^{5}\) km) and subtends about 0.5°, while a star, though enormously larger, is so far away that it subtends a vanishingly small angle. Hence the moon looks bigger.

Concepts to recall

  • Visual angle ≈ size/distance (radians).
  • Optical instruments work by increasing the visual angle.

Q2(a)Electrostatics[4]

With the help of a labelled diagram, obtain an expression for the electric field intensity 'E' at a point P in broad side position (i.e. equatorial plane) of an electric dipole.

Answer

Dipole: charges \(-q\) at A and \(+q\) at B, \(AB = 2l\), dipole moment \(p = q\cdot2l\) directed from \(-q\) to \(+q\). P is on the perpendicular bisector at distance \(r\) from the centre O.

Distance of P from each charge \(= \sqrt{r^2+l^2}\), so the magnitudes are equal:

\[E_+ = E_- = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q}{r^2+l^2}\]

\(\vec E_+\) points along BP (away from +q); \(\vec E_-\) points along PA (towards −q). Resolve each along and perpendicular to the axis. The components perpendicular to AB (\(E\sin\theta\)) are equal and opposite and cancel. The components parallel to AB (\(E\cos\theta\)) are both directed from +q towards −q and add:

\[E = E_+\cos\theta + E_-\cos\theta = 2\,\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2+l^2}\cdot\frac{l}{\sqrt{r^2+l^2}}\qquad\left(\cos\theta=\frac{l}{\sqrt{r^2+l^2}}\right)\] \[E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{q\,(2l)}{(r^2+l^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{p}{(r^2+l^2)^{3/2}}\]

Direction: parallel to the axis, from +q towards −q, i.e. opposite to \(\vec p\).

For a short dipole (\(r\gg l\)): \(\vec E = -\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{\vec p}{r^3}\)

Concepts to recall

  • Field of a point charge \(kq/r^2\); add vectorially.
  • Equatorial field is half the axial field (\(2kp/r^3\)) and antiparallel to \(\vec p\).

Q6(c)Optics — Ray Optics[3]

A thin biconvex lens of refractive index 3/2 is placed on a horizontal plane mirror as shown below. The space between the lens and the mirror is then filled with water of refractive index 4/3. It is found that when a point object is placed 15 cm above the lens on its principal axis, the object coincides with its own image. On repeating with another liquid, the object and the image again coincide at a distance 25 cm from the lens. Calculate the refractive index of the liquid.

Answer

Principle. The object coincides with its own image when the rays, after passing through the lens system, fall normally on the plane mirror and retrace their path — i.e. when the object is at the focus of the combined system. So the object distance equals the focal length of the combination of the glass lens and the liquid between lens and mirror.

The liquid forms a plano-concave lens whose curved face has the radius R of the lower face of the glass lens (taken as an equiconvex lens of radii R).

Glass lens: \(\dfrac1{f_1}=(\mu_g-1)\left(\dfrac1R+\dfrac1R\right)=\left(\tfrac32-1\right)\dfrac2R=\dfrac1R\)

Water lens: \(\dfrac1{f_2}=(\mu_w-1)\left(-\dfrac1R+\dfrac1\infty\right)=-\dfrac{1}{3R}\)

Combination: \(\dfrac1{15}=\dfrac1R-\dfrac1{3R}=\dfrac{2}{3R}\Rightarrow R = 10\) cm (so \(f_1 = 10\) cm).

With the liquid of index μ: \(\dfrac1{25}=\dfrac1{10}-\dfrac{\mu-1}{10}\Rightarrow\mu-1 = 10\left(\dfrac1{10}-\dfrac1{25}\right)=0.6\)

\(\mu_{\text{liquid}} = 1.6\)

Concepts to recall

  • Lens maker: \(\frac1f=(\mu-1)\left(\frac1{R_1}-\frac1{R_2}\right)\).
  • Lenses in contact: \(\frac1F=\frac1{f_1}+\frac1{f_2}\).
  • Object = own image on a plane mirror ⇒ object at the focus.
Q7(a)Optics — Wave Optics[2]

Two students are separated by a 7 m partition wall in a room 10 m high. If both light and sound waves can bend around obstacles, how is it that the students are unable to see each other even though they can converse easily?

Answer

Bending (diffraction) is appreciable only when the size of the obstacle/opening is comparable to the wavelength.

Sound waves of speech have wavelengths of about 0.3 m to a few metres, comparable with the 3 m gap above the 7 m wall, so sound diffracts round the wall and is heard.

Light has \(\lambda\approx5\times10^{-7}\) m, about \(10^{-7}\) times the size of the wall; its diffraction is negligible and it travels in practically straight lines, so the wall casts a sharp shadow and the students cannot see each other.

Concepts to recall

  • Diffraction significant when \(a\sim\lambda\); angular spread \(\sim\lambda/a\).
Q7(b)Optics — Wave Optics[3]

Consider interference between waves from the two sources of intensities I and 4I. Find intensities at points where the phase difference is (a) π/2 (b) π

Answer

For two coherent waves, \(I_R = I_1 + I_2 + 2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\phi\), with \(I_1 = I,\ I_2 = 4I,\ 2\sqrt{I_1I_2} = 4I\).

(a) \(\phi = \pi/2\): \(\cos\phi = 0\) ⇒ \(I_R = I + 4I = 5I\)

(b) \(\phi = \pi\): \(\cos\phi = -1\) ⇒ \(I_R = 5I - 4I\) ⇒ \(I_R = I\)

Concepts to recall

  • \(I\propto a^2\); \(I_R = I_1+I_2+2\sqrt{I_1I_2}\cos\phi\).
  • \(I_{\max}=(\sqrt{I_1}+\sqrt{I_2})^2\), \(I_{\min}=(\sqrt{I_1}-\sqrt{I_2})^2\).

Electrons having a velocity v of \(2\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{ms^{-1}}\) pass undeviated through a uniform electric field E of intensity \(5\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{Vm^{-1}}\) and a uniform magnetic field B.

i) Find the magnitude of magnetic flux density B of the magnetic fields.

ii) What is the direction of B, if v is towards right and E is vertically downwards in the plane of this paper?

Q8(c)Atoms and Nuclei — Atoms[3]

Energy levels of H atom are given by: \(E_n = -13.6/n^2\) eV, where n is Principal Quantum number. Calculate the wavelength of electromagnetic radiation by hydrogen atom resulting from the transition: n = 2 to n = 1.

Answer

\(\Delta E = E_2 - E_1 = -\dfrac{13.6}{4} - \left(-13.6\right) = 10.2\ \mathrm{eV} = 10.2\times1.6\times10^{-19} = 1.632\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm J\)

\(\lambda \approx 1.21\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm m = 121\ \mathrm{nm}\) (ultraviolet; first line of the Lyman series)

Concepts to recall

  • \(h\nu = E_i - E_f\), \(\lambda = hc/\Delta E\).
  • Transitions to n = 1: Lyman (UV); to n = 2: Balmer (visible).
Q9(c)Atoms and Nuclei — Nuclei[2]

Calculate the energy released when an electron annihilates a positron.

Answer

\(e^- + e^+ \rightarrow 2\gamma\); the whole rest mass \(2m_e\) is converted into energy.

\(E = 2m_ec^2 = 2\times9\times10^{-31}\times(3\times10^{8})^2 = 1.62\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm J\)

\(= \dfrac{1.62\times10^{-13}}{1.6\times10^{-19}}\ \mathrm{eV}\) ⇒ \(E \approx 1.62\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm J \approx 1.01\ \mathrm{MeV}\)

With the accurate mass \(9.11\times10^{-31}\) kg the value is 1.02 MeV (0.511 MeV per particle).

Concepts to recall

  • \(E = mc^2\); \(m_ec^2 = 0.511\) MeV.
  • Annihilation gives two γ-photons (momentum conservation).
Q10(a)Electronic Devices[3]

Draw a labelled energy band diagram for a solid which is an insulator. What is the main difference between the diagram and that of a semi-conductor?

Answer

In an insulator the valence band is completely filled, the conduction band is empty and they are separated by a wide forbidden gap \(E_g > 3\) eV (≈ 6 eV for diamond). No electron can gain enough energy at room temperature to cross it, so there is no conduction.

Difference: in a semiconductor the forbidden gap is small (≈ 1 eV: Si 1.1 eV, Ge 0.7 eV), so at room temperature some electrons are thermally excited into the conduction band, leaving holes in the valence band, and the material conducts slightly.

Concepts to recall

  • Conductor: bands overlap; semiconductor: \(E_g\lesssim3\) eV; insulator: \(E_g>3\) eV.
  • Typical gaps: C 5.4–6 eV, Si 1.1 eV, Ge 0.7 eV.

Why left out: Diffraction grating — not in ISC 2027: Wave Optics lists only 'Fraunhofer diffraction due to a single slit' (a sin θ = nλ); a grating appears nowhere in the syllabus.

Q1(B)(xiii) [1]

For an achromatic combination of two lenses in contact, why should the lenses be of opposite nature?

Why left out: Achromatic combination of lenses — not in ISC 2027: Optics 6(c) lists only 'Dispersion; Angular dispersion; dispersive power, rainbow'; achromatism (ω₁/f₁ + ω₂/f₂ = 0) is not listed.

Q7(c) [3]

Briefly describe how a given beam of light can be analysed and determined if it is linearly polarised or unpolarised. What is double refraction?

Why left out: Polarisation and double refraction — not in ISC 2027: Wave Optics covers Huygens' principle, interference (YDSE) and single-slit diffraction; polarisation occurs only as a word in Unit 7 ('wave nature … exhibited in interference, diffraction and polarisation'), with no Polaroid, Malus, Brewster or double refraction.

(i) What is Compton effect?

(ii) In Coolidge X-Ray tube (Modern X-Ray tube) how will you vary: (1) Intensity of emitted X-Rays? (2) Penetrating power of emitted X-Rays?

Why left out: Compton effect and the Coolidge X-ray tube — not in ISC 2027: Unit 7 covers only the photoelectric effect and matter waves; X-rays appear only as a band of the EM spectrum (Unit 5, 'elementary facts' of source, detection and uses), not the X-ray tube and the control of intensity and hardness, which belonged to the deleted X-rays section.
