Q1(A)(i)Electronic Devices[1]

In an N-type semiconductor, there is

(a) a majority of free electrons   (b) a majority of holes   (c) an equal number of holes and free electrons   (d) a trivalent element as an impurity

Answer

(a) a majority of free electrons.

N-type silicon/germanium is doped with a pentavalent (donor) impurity such as P or As; four of its valence electrons form covalent bonds and the fifth becomes a free electron. Electrons are the majority carriers, holes the minority carriers. (A trivalent impurity would make it p-type.)

Concepts to recall

  • n-type: pentavalent donor (P, As, Sb) ⇒ \(n_e \gg n_h\).
  • p-type: trivalent acceptor (B, Al, In) ⇒ \(n_h \gg n_e\).
Q1(A)(ii)Electronic Devices[1]

Zener diode is used as:

(a) Half wave rectifier   (b) full wave rectifier   (c) voltage regulator   (d) an amplifier

Answer

(c) voltage regulator.

In reverse breakdown the voltage across a Zener diode stays practically constant at \(V_Z\) over a wide range of current, so connected in reverse bias across a load (with a series resistor) it holds the load voltage steady.

Concepts to recall

  • Zener: heavily doped, used in reverse breakdown; \(V_Z\) constant.
  • Series resistor drops the excess input voltage.
Q1(A)(iii)Current Electricity[1]

A circuit contains an ammeter, a battery of 30 V and a resistance of 40.8 ohm all connected in series. If the ammeter has a coil of resistance 480 ohm and a shunt of 20 ohm, the reading in the ammeter will be:

(a) 0.25 A   (b) 2 A   (c) 1 A   (d) 0.5 A

Answer

(d) 0.5 A

Resistance of the ammeter = coil ∥ shunt \(= \dfrac{480\times20}{480+20} = 19.2\ \Omega\).

Total resistance \(= 40.8 + 19.2 = 60\ \Omega\); \(I = \dfrac{30}{60} = 0.5\) A, which is what the ammeter reads.

Concepts to recall

  • Ammeter = galvanometer with a low shunt in parallel: \(R_A = GS/(G+S)\).
  • Ohm's law for the whole circuit \(I = E/R_{\text{total}}\).
Q1(A)(iv)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[1]

When the current in a coil change from 8 A to 2 A in \(3\times10^{-2}\) second, the emf induced in the coil is 2 V. The self-inductance of the coil, in millihenry is:

(a) 1   (b) 5   (c) 20   (d) 10

(d) 10

Q1(A)(v)Optics — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments[1]

Assertion (A): The graph for 'v' versus 'u' of a concave mirror forming real images, falls within the second quadrant.

Reason (R): For concave mirrors, according to the sign convention for real images, 'u' is negative, and 'v' is positive.

(a) Both Assertion and Reason are true, and Reason is the correct explanation for Assertion.
(b) Both Assertion and Reason are true, but Reason is not the correct explanation for Assertion.
(c) Assertion is true, and Reason is false.
(d) Both Assertion and Reason are false.

Answer

(d) Both Assertion and Reason are false.

In the Cartesian (New Cartesian) convention, distances are measured from the pole and the incident light travels in the +x direction. For a concave mirror forming a real image, the object and the image are both in front of the mirror, so u and v are both negative. The \(v\)–\(u\) graph is a rectangular hyperbola lying in the third quadrant, with asymptotes \(u = f\) and \(v = f\) and passing through \((2f, 2f)\).

So the Assertion (second quadrant) is false and the Reason (v positive) is false.

Concepts to recall

  • Mirror formula \(\dfrac1v + \dfrac1u = \dfrac1f\); concave \(f\lt 0\).
  • Real image of a real object: both on the incident side ⇒ same sign as \(u\).
Q1(A)(vi)Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter[1]

Variation of photoelectric current with intensity of light is best represented by:

Answer

(d) — a straight line through the origin.

For a given frequency above threshold, the number of photoelectrons emitted per second is proportional to the number of photons falling per second, i.e. to the intensity. So the (saturation) photocurrent \(\propto\) intensity: a straight line through the origin, with no saturation in intensity.

Concepts to recall

  • Photocurrent ∝ intensity (ν fixed, ν > ν₀).
  • Maximum KE depends on ν, not on intensity.

Q1(B)(i)Atoms and Nuclei — Atoms[1]

State one limitation of Bohr's atomic model.

Answer

Any one of:

  • It applies only to hydrogen and hydrogen-like (one-electron) ions such as He\(^+\), Li\(^{2+}\); it fails for many-electron atoms.
  • It cannot explain the relative intensities of spectral lines, or their fine structure (each line is really a group of close lines).
  • It does not explain the splitting of lines in a magnetic field (Zeeman effect) or an electric field (Stark effect).

Concepts to recall

  • Bohr's postulates: stationary orbits, \(mvr = nh/2\pi\), \(h\nu = E_i - E_f\).
Q1(B)(ii)Dual Nature of Radiation and Matter[1]

In an experiment of photoelectric effect, the slope of cutoff voltage versus frequency of incident light is found to be \(4.12\times10^{-15}\) V s. Calculate the value of Planck's constant.

Answer

\(eV_0 = h\nu - \phi_0 \Rightarrow V_0 = \dfrac{h}{e}\nu - \dfrac{\phi_0}{e}\), so slope \(= h/e\).

\(h = e\times\text{slope} = 1.6\times10^{-19}\times4.12\times10^{-15}\)

\(h = 6.59\times10^{-34}\) J s

Concepts to recall

  • Einstein: \(eV_0 = h\nu - \phi_0\).
  • \(V_0\)–\(\nu\) graph: slope \(h/e\), intercept \(\nu_0\) on the ν-axis.
Q1(B)(iii)Optics — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments[1]

Give an advantage of reflecting telescopes over refracting telescopes.

Answer

Any one: a mirror objective has no chromatic aberration (reflection does not depend on wavelength); a parabolic mirror is free from spherical aberration; a large-aperture mirror is easier and cheaper to make and support than a large lens, so it gathers more light and has greater resolving power.

Concepts to recall

  • Resolving power of a telescope ∝ aperture D / λ.
  • Lenses disperse light (chromatic aberration); mirrors do not.

Q1(B)(v)Atoms and Nuclei — Atoms[1]

If a hydrogen atom goes from the 3rd excited state to 2nd excited state, what kind of radiation is emitted?

Answer

3rd excited state is \(n = 4\); 2nd excited state is \(n = 3\). A transition ending on \(n = 3\) belongs to the Paschen series.

\(\dfrac1\lambda = R\left(\dfrac1{3^2}-\dfrac1{4^2}\right) = \dfrac{7R}{144} \Rightarrow \lambda = \dfrac{144}{7\times1.097\times10^{7}} = 1.88\times10^{-6}\) m ≈ 1875 nm.

Infrared radiation (Paschen series, λ ≈ 1875 nm).

Concepts to recall

  • Ground state n = 1; k-th excited state n = k + 1.
  • Lyman (→1) UV, Balmer (→2) visible, Paschen (→3) IR.
Q1(B)(vi)Atoms and Nuclei — Nuclei[1]

Name the particles and radiation involved in a pair production. Give equation relating them.

Answer

A γ-ray photon passing close to a nucleus converts into an electron and a positron:

\(\gamma \;\rightarrow\; {}_{-1}^{\;\;0}e + {}_{+1}^{\;\;0}e\),   with \(h\nu = 2m_0c^2 + K_{e^-} + K_{e^+}\), so \(h\nu \ge 2m_0c^2 = 1.02\) MeV.

Concepts to recall

  • \(E = mc^2\); rest energy of an electron = 0.511 MeV.
  • Annihilation is the reverse: \(e^- + e^+ \to 2\gamma\).
Q1(B)(vii)Atoms and Nuclei — Nuclei[1]

In a nuclear reactor what are cadmium rods used for?

Answer

Cadmium rods are the control rods: cadmium absorbs neutrons strongly, so pushing the rods in or pulling them out controls the number of neutrons available for fission and keeps the chain reaction steady (multiplication factor \(k = 1\)); fully inserted they shut the reactor down.

Concepts to recall

  • Reactor parts: fuel, moderator (slows neutrons), control rods (absorb), coolant, shielding.

Q2Electrostatics[2]

The electric potential as a function of distance x as shown in the figure. Construct a graph of electric field strength E as a function of distance x.

Answer

\(E = -\dfrac{dV}{dx}\), i.e. minus the slope of the V–x graph. Let the maximum potential be \(V_0\) and x be in metres.

0 < x < 1: V rises uniformly from 0 to \(V_0\), slope \(= +V_0\) ⇒ \(E = -V_0\) (constant).
1 < x < 2: V constant ⇒ \(E = 0\).
2 < x < 3: V falls uniformly from \(V_0\) to 0, slope \(= -V_0\) ⇒ \(E = +V_0\) (constant).

[The paper gives no value on the V-axis, so the field is expressed in terms of the maximum potential \(V_0\); E has the unit V m\(^{-1}\).]

Concepts to recall

  • \(E = -dV/dx\): field points towards decreasing potential.
  • Uniform slope ⇒ uniform field; constant V ⇒ E = 0.

If there are identical capacitors all connected in parallel connected to V volt source, then find the energy stored in the system.

[The number of capacitors and their capacitance are missing from the printed question; taken as \(n\) capacitors, each of capacitance \(C\).]

Answer

In parallel, \(C_{\text{eq}} = C + C + \dots\) (n terms) \(= nC\), and each capacitor has the full p.d. V.

\(U = \tfrac12 C_{\text{eq}}V^2\) ⇒ \(U = \tfrac12\,nCV^2\)

(Equivalently, n times the energy \(\tfrac12CV^2\) of one capacitor.)

Q4(a)Current Electricity[2]

A heater takes 20 minutes to boil a given amount of water. Its coil is cut and (¼)th of its length is used in the heater now. How much time will it take now to boil the same amount of water using the same source beginning with the same initial temperature?

Answer

The heat needed, \(H\), is the same. With the same source voltage V, \(H = \dfrac{V^2}{R}\,t\) ⇒ \(t \propto R\).

\(R \propto l\): using \(\tfrac14\) of the length, \(R' = R/4\).

\(t' = t\,\dfrac{R'}{R} = 20\times\dfrac14\) ⇒ \(t' = 5\) minutes

Concepts to recall

  • \(P = V^2/R\) at constant V; \(H = Pt\).
  • \(R = \rho l/A\).

Q7Atoms and Nuclei — Atoms[2]

Show that the shortest wavelengths in Lyman, Balmer and Paschen series have their wavelengths in the ratio of 1 : 4 : 9.

Answer

\(\dfrac1\lambda = R\left(\dfrac1{n_f^2} - \dfrac1{n_i^2}\right)\). The shortest wavelength (series limit) is for \(n_i \to \infty\): \(\dfrac1{\lambda_{\min}} = \dfrac{R}{n_f^2}\) ⇒ \(\lambda_{\min} = \dfrac{n_f^2}{R}\).

Lyman \(n_f = 1\): \(\lambda_L = 1/R\); Balmer \(n_f = 2\): \(\lambda_B = 4/R\); Paschen \(n_f = 3\): \(\lambda_P = 9/R\).

\(\lambda_L : \lambda_B : \lambda_P = 1 : 4 : 9\)

Concepts to recall

  • Rydberg formula \(1/\lambda = R(1/n_f^2 - 1/n_i^2)\).
  • Series limit ⇒ \(n_i = \infty\).

Q11(b)Magnetic Effects of Current and Magnetism[3]

(OR) Using Ampere's circuital law, obtain an expression for the magnetic flux density 'B' at a point 'X' at a perpendicular distance 'r' from a long current carrying conductor.

Answer

Ampere's circuital law: \(\oint \vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0 I\), where I is the net current threading the closed loop.

Take as the Amperian loop a circle of radius r through X, in a plane perpendicular to the wire, centred on it.

By symmetry B has the same magnitude at every point of the loop and is tangential to it, so \(\vec B \parallel d\vec l\) and \(\vec B\cdot d\vec l = B\,dl\).

\(\oint \vec B\cdot d\vec l = B\oint dl = B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I\)

\(B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\), along the tangent to the circle; direction by the right-hand grip rule.

Concepts to recall

  • \(\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}\).
  • Choose a loop on which B is constant and parallel to dl.
Q12(a)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[3]

In series LCR circuit using phasor diagram, derive an expression for impedance.

Answer

Let \(I = I_0\sin\omega t\) flow through R, L and C in series (same current in all). Peak voltages: \(V_R = I_0R\) in phase with I; \(V_L = I_0X_L\) leading I by 90°; \(V_C = I_0X_C\) lagging I by 90°, where \(X_L = \omega L\), \(X_C = 1/\omega C\).

Draw I along the x-axis. \(V_L\) and \(V_C\) are opposite, so their resultant is \(V_L - V_C\) (taking \(X_L \gt X_C\)) along +y; the applied voltage \(V_0\) is the phasor sum of \(V_R\) and \(V_L - V_C\).

\(V_0^2 = V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2 = I_0^2\left[R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2\right]\)

Impedance \(Z = \dfrac{V_0}{I_0}\) ⇒ \(Z = \sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}\)

Phase angle: \(\tan\phi = \dfrac{V_L - V_C}{V_R} = \dfrac{X_L - X_C}{R}\), voltage leading the current by φ.

Concepts to recall

  • \(X_L = \omega L\) (V leads I by 90°), \(X_C = 1/\omega C\) (V lags by 90°).
  • Phasors add as vectors.
Q12(b)Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[3]

(OR) In series LCR circuit draw I vs ω graph and label the bandwidth, resonant frequency and current amplitude.

Answer

\(I_0 = \dfrac{V_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2}}\): the current is maximum (\(= V_0/R\)) at the resonant frequency \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\), where \(X_L = X_C\), and falls on either side.

Current amplitude at resonance: \(I_0 = V_0/R\) (the peak). Resonant frequency: \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\). Bandwidth: \(\Delta\omega = \omega_2 - \omega_1\), where \(\omega_1, \omega_2\) are the frequencies at which the current falls to \(I_0/\sqrt2\) (half-power points); \(\Delta\omega = R/L\). A smaller R gives a sharper peak (higher Q = \(\omega_0/\Delta\omega\)).

Concepts to recall

  • Resonance: \(X_L = X_C\), Z = R, \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\).
  • Half-power points at \(I_0/\sqrt2\); Q = \(\omega_0/\Delta\omega\).
Q13Optics — Wave Optics[3]

Student 1 and Student 2 conducted experiments using coherent sources of light, with Student 1 using a single slit and Student 2 using a double slit.

Answer the following questions.

(a) Identify the patterns resulting from Student 1 and Student 2 experiments.

(b) Give two reasons that assisted you in recognizing the patterns.

Answer

(a) Student 1 (single slit) — pattern C, a diffraction pattern. Student 2 (double slit) — pattern B, an interference pattern.

(b) 1. Pattern C has a much wider, very bright central maximum with secondary maxima that are narrower and far fainter (intensity falls off rapidly) — the signature of single-slit diffraction (central maximum is twice as wide as the others).
2. Pattern B has equally spaced fringes of equal width and nearly equal brightness — the signature of two-source interference, where fringe width \(\beta = \lambda D/d\) is constant.

(Pattern A, whose rings crowd closer together outward, is neither.)

This is CISCE Competency-Focused Practice Question 92 (parts (a) and (b)). Its published key: "Student 1 – Pattern C; Student 2 – Pattern B", the reasons being the wider central maximum with fringe widths diminishing outward (diffraction, C) and the uniform fringe widths (interference, B).

Concepts to recall

  • Interference: equal fringe width \(\beta = \lambda D/d\), equal intensity.
  • Single-slit diffraction: central maximum width \(2\lambda D/a\), intensity falls rapidly.
Q14Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[3]

Match the following:

1.\(\omega L \gt \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\)(i)The current is in phase with voltage V.
2.\(\omega L \lt \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\)(ii)The current lags in phase with voltage V.

Q15Electromagnetic Induction and Alternating Currents[3]

AB and CD are two parallel conductors kept 1 m apart and connected by a resistance R of 6 ohm, as shown in the figure below. They are placed in a magnetic field \(B = 3\times10^{-2}\) T which is perpendicular to the plane of the conductors and directed into the paper. A wire MN is placed over AB and CD and then made to slide with velocity 2 m/s. (Neglect the resistance of AB, CD and MN.) Calculate the induced current flowing through the resistor R.

Answer

Motional emf: \(e = Blv = 3\times10^{-2}\times1\times2 = 0.06\) V.

\(I = \dfrac{e}{R} = \dfrac{0.06}{6}\) ⇒ \(I = 0.01\) A \(= 10\) mA

Direction (taking MN moving to the right, away from R): the area of loop AMNC and the inward flux increase, so by Lenz's law the induced current makes an outward field — anticlockwise as seen: from N to M in the rod, and from A to C through R (Fleming's right-hand rule gives the same).

Q17Optics — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments[3]

In an optics experiment, an optics student is using a compound microscope. The examiner gives the requisite instructions and asks the student some questions. State whether the responses of the student, in each case, is correct or incorrect. Give a reason for your answer.

i) Examiner: increase the magnification produced by the objective lens.
Student: Decrease the distance between object and objective lens, keeping in mind that this distance must be greater than the focal length of the objective lens.

ii) Examiner: Increase the resolving power of the instrument.
Student: Illuminates the object with the light of longer wavelength.

iii) Examiner: Which of the two lenses among f1, f2 and f3 will you prefer to take as an objective and eyepiece of compound microscope? (f1 = 2 cm, f2 = 100 cm and f3 = 6 cm)
Student: f1 = 2 cm as an objective and f2 = 100 cm as an eyepiece.

Answer

(i) Correct. \(m_o = \dfrac{v_o}{u_o} = \dfrac{f_o}{u_o - f_o}\) (magnitudes): as the object is brought closer to \(F_o\) (still outside it, so the image stays real), \(v_o\) increases and \(u_o\) decreases, so the magnification increases.

(ii) Incorrect. Resolving power of a microscope \(= \dfrac{2n\sin\theta}{1.22\lambda}\propto \dfrac1\lambda\); it is increased by using light of shorter wavelength (e.g. blue/UV).

(iii) Incorrect (partly). The objective f1 = 2 cm is right, but the eyepiece should also have a short focal length — f3 = 6 cm — since \(M = \dfrac{v_o}{u_o}\left(1 + \dfrac{D}{f_e}\right)\) is larger for a smaller \(f_e\). A 100 cm lens would give almost no eyepiece magnification (it suits a telescope objective).

Concepts to recall

  • Compound microscope: \(M = m_o m_e\), \(m_e = 1 + D/f_e\); both \(f_o\), \(f_e\) small, \(f_o \lt f_e\).
  • Resolving power ∝ 1/λ.

Q18(a)Optics — Ray Optics and Optical Instruments[5]

I) For 2 thin lenses kept in contact with each other show that \(\dfrac1F = \dfrac1{f_1} + \dfrac1{f_2}\).

II) Calculate the critical angle for glass-air interface, if a ray of light incident on the interface is deviated through 15 degree when its angle of incidence is 40 degree.

Answer

I) Let thin lenses L1 (focal length \(f_1\)) and L2 (\(f_2\)) be in contact on a common axis, and O a point object at distance u.

L1 alone would form the image I1 at \(v_1\): \(\dfrac1{v_1} - \dfrac1u = \dfrac1{f_1}\) … (1)

I1 acts as the (virtual) object for L2, which forms the final image I at v. The lenses are thin and in contact, so distances are measured from the same point: \(\dfrac1v - \dfrac1{v_1} = \dfrac1{f_2}\) … (2)

Adding (1) and (2): \(\dfrac1v - \dfrac1u = \dfrac1{f_1} + \dfrac1{f_2}\).

If the combination is a single lens of focal length F, \(\dfrac1v - \dfrac1u = \dfrac1F\), so \(\dfrac1F = \dfrac1{f_1} + \dfrac1{f_2}\) (and \(P = P_1 + P_2\)).

II) The ray is incident from air on the glass at \(i = 40^\circ\). Entering the denser medium it bends towards the normal, so the angle of refraction is \(r = i - \delta = 40^\circ - 15^\circ = 25^\circ\).

Snell's law: \(n_g = \dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \dfrac{\sin40^\circ}{\sin25^\circ} = \dfrac{0.6428}{0.4226} = 1.521\)

The critical angle C (for light going from glass to air, at the same interface): \(\sin C = \dfrac1{n_g} = \dfrac{1}{1.521} = 0.6575\) ⇒ \(C \approx 41.1^\circ\)

[The question does not say on which side the ray starts. The data decide it: taking the ray inside the glass (bending away, r = 55°) would give \(n = \sin55^\circ/\sin40^\circ = 1.27\), far below any glass, whereas the air-to-glass reading gives n = 1.52, the refractive index of ordinary glass. (A glass of n = 1.52 could not even give a 15° deviation from inside at 40°: the ray would emerge at 77.9°.) The critical angle is a property of the interface, so it is found either way from \(\sin C = 1/n\).]

Concepts to recall

  • Lens formula \(1/v - 1/u = 1/f\) (Cartesian).
  • Image by the first lens = object for the second.
  • Snell: \(n = \sin i/\sin r\); entering a denser medium the ray bends towards the normal, \(\delta = i - r\).
  • \(\sin C = 1/n\) (denser → rarer).
Q18(b)Optics — Wave Optics[5]

(OR) i) Derive laws of reflection using Huygen's principle.

ii) If you had to construct an optical fibre using the two materials, which would you prefer for the core and the cladding? X: with a refractive index of 1.4905 or Y: with a refractive index of 1.337? Give reason in support of your answer.

Answer

(i) Huygens' principle: every point on a wavefront is a source of secondary wavelets spreading with the speed of the wave; the new wavefront is the forward envelope (common tangent) of these wavelets.

A plane wavefront AB strikes the reflecting surface XY, A touching it first, at angle of incidence i. While the disturbance at B travels to C (BC = ct), the secondary wavelet from A spreads to a hemisphere of radius AD = ct. The tangent CD from C to this wavelet is the reflected wavefront.

In right-angled triangles ABC and CDA (right angles at B and D): AC is common and BC = AD = ct, so the triangles are congruent ⇒ ∠BAC = ∠DCA.

∠BAC is the angle between the incident wavefront and the surface = angle of incidence i; ∠DCA = angle between the reflected wavefront and the surface = angle of reflection r. Hence \(i = r\). Also the incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the plane of the figure — the second law.

(ii) Core: X (n = 1.4905); cladding: Y (n = 1.337). Light is guided by total internal reflection at the core–cladding boundary, which needs light travelling in the optically denser medium, so the core must have the higher refractive index. Critical angle \(\sin C = \dfrac{1.337}{1.4905} = 0.897\), C ≈ 63.8°: rays striking the boundary at more than 63.8° are totally reflected and travel along the fibre.

Concepts to recall

  • Huygens: secondary wavelets; new wavefront = envelope.
  • TIR needs denser → rarer and angle > C; \(\sin C = n_2/n_1\).
Q19(a)Atoms and Nuclei — Atoms[5]

Using Bohr's postulates derive an expression for velocity of electrons in the hydrogen atom and prove that it is 1/137 times the speed of light.

Answer

Postulates used: (1) the electron moves in a circular orbit, the Coulomb attraction of the nucleus providing the centripetal force; (2) only those orbits are allowed in which the angular momentum is an integral multiple of \(h/2\pi\): \(mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}\).

(1): \(\dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{e^2}{r^2}\) ⇒ \(mv^2r = \dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\) … (i)

(2): \(mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}\) … (ii)

Dividing (i) by (ii): \(v_n = \dfrac{e^2}{2\varepsilon_0 nh}\)

For the first orbit (n = 1): \(\dfrac{v_1}{c} = \dfrac{e^2}{2\varepsilon_0hc} = \dfrac{(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{2\times8.85\times10^{-12}\times6.6\times10^{-34}\times3\times10^{8}} = \dfrac{2.56\times10^{-38}}{3.505\times10^{-36}} = 7.30\times10^{-3}\)

\(\dfrac{v_1}{c} = \dfrac{1}{137}\) ⇒ \(v_1 = \dfrac{c}{137} \approx 2.19\times10^{6}\) m s\(^{-1}\)

(The ratio \(e^2/2\varepsilon_0hc\) is the fine-structure constant; in general \(v_n = \dfrac{c}{137\,n}\).)

Concepts to recall

  • Centripetal force = Coulomb force.
  • \(mvr = nh/2\pi\).
  • \(v_n \propto 1/n\), \(r_n \propto n^2\).
Q19(b)(i)Atoms and Nuclei — Nuclei[3]

(OR) Sketch a graph showing the variation of binding energy per nucleon of a nucleus with mass number. Indicate which regions of this graph shows stable, nuclear fission prone and nuclear fusion prone nuclei.

Answer

BE/A rises steeply for light nuclei (with peaks at \(^4\)He, \(^{12}\)C, \(^{16}\)O), reaches a broad maximum of about 8.8 MeV near A ≈ 56 (Fe), then falls slowly to about 7.6 MeV for A ≈ 238.

Stable: the flat top, A ≈ 40 to 120 (highest BE/A, most tightly bound).
Fusion-prone: light nuclei, A ≲ 20–30 — joining two of them gives a nucleus of higher BE/A, releasing energy.
Fission-prone: heavy nuclei, A ≳ 170 (e.g. U-235) — splitting into two middle nuclei raises BE/A, releasing energy.

Concepts to recall

  • BE = Δm c²; BE/A measures stability.
  • Energy released whenever products have higher BE/A.

Q20Electronic Devices[5]

A rectifier is an electronic circuit that converts alternating current (AC) into direct current (DC). There are two main types of rectifiers — half-wave and full-wave.

In a half-wave rectifier, only one half of the input AC signal (either positive or negative) is allowed to pass through the circuit, while the other half is blocked. It uses a single diode and produces an output that is pulsating DC. However, since only half of the input wave is utilized, its efficiency is low, and the output is not smooth.

In a full-wave rectifier, both halves of the AC input are used to produce DC. This can be done using either two diodes with a center-tapped transformer or four diodes in a bridge arrangement. The output of a full-wave rectifier is smoother and has higher efficiency compared to a half-wave rectifier. Therefore, full-wave rectifiers are more commonly used in power supply circuits.

a) State how the thickness of depletion region changes forward and reverse biasing a p-n junction.

b) Draw a neat, labelled diagram of a full wave rectifier.

c) Show the input and output signals in it.

Answer

(a) Forward bias: the applied field opposes the barrier field; majority carriers are pushed towards the junction, so the depletion region becomes thinner (barrier lowered). Reverse bias: the applied field adds to the barrier field; carriers are drawn away from the junction, so the depletion region becomes wider (barrier raised).

(b) Centre-tap full-wave rectifier (the form in the ISC syllabus; the bridge is not required):

During the half-cycle when S1 is positive with respect to the centre tap T, D1 is forward biased and conducts, D2 is reverse biased; current flows S1 → D1 → P → RL → T. In the next half-cycle S2 is positive, D2 conducts and D1 is off; current again flows P → RL → T. So the current through RL is in the same direction in both halves.

(c)

The output is unidirectional (pulsating DC) with one positive pulse for every half-cycle of the input: its frequency is twice the input frequency.

Concepts to recall

  • Forward bias narrows, reverse bias widens the depletion layer.
  • Centre-tap FWR: two diodes conduct on alternate half-cycles; output ripple frequency = 2f.
