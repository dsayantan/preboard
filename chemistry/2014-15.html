Q1(b)(iii)Electrochemistry[1]

(iii) The standard reduction potentials for \(\ce{Zn^2+/Zn}\), \(\ce{Ni^2+/Ni}\) and \(\ce{Fe^2+/Fe}\) are −0.76, −0.23 and −0.44 V respectively. The reaction \(\ce{X + Y^2+ -> X^2+ + Y}\) will be spontaneous when:

(a) X = Ni, Y = Zn (b) X = Fe, Y = Zn (c) X = Zn, Y = Ni (d) X = Ni, Y = Fe.

Answer

X is oxidised (anode), \(\ce{Y^2+}\) is reduced (cathode); spontaneous if \(E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_{\ce{Y}} - E^\circ_{\ce{X}} > 0\).

(a) −0.76 − (−0.23) = −0.53 V; (b) −0.76 − (−0.44) = −0.32 V; (c) −0.23 − (−0.76) = +0.53 V; (d) −0.44 − (−0.23) = −0.21 V.

(c) X = Zn, Y = Ni (\(E^\circ = +0.53\) V, \(\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ < 0\))

Concepts to recall

  • \(E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode}\); spontaneous when positive.
  • A metal lower in the electrochemical series (more negative \(E^\circ\)) displaces one above it.
Q1(b)(v)d- and f-Block Elements[1]

(v) Lanthanide Contraction is due to increase in (a) Shielding effect (b) Atomic No. (c) Size of 4f Orbital (d) Effective nuclear charge

Answer

(d) Effective nuclear charge

Across the lanthanoids each added proton is matched by an electron entering the 4f subshell; 4f electrons shield one another poorly (diffuse shape), so the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons rises steadily and the atomic/ionic radii decrease from La to Lu.

Concepts to recall

  • Lanthanoid contraction: steady decrease in size La → Lu due to imperfect shielding by 4f electrons.
  • Consequences: Zr/Hf nearly equal size; basicity of \(\ce{Ln(OH)3}\) decreases La → Lu.
Q1(c)(i)Chemical Kinetics[1]

Answer the following questions:

(i) A reaction is 50% complete in 2 hours and 75% complete in 4 hours. What is the order of the reaction?

Answer

50% → 2 h: first half-life = 2 h. 75% means half of the remaining 50% has also reacted in the next 2 h: the second half-life is also 2 h. The half-life does not depend on the concentration left, which is true only for a first-order reaction (\(t_{1/2} = 0.693/k\)).

Check: \(k = \frac{2.303}{2}\log\frac{100}{50} = 0.3466\ \text{h}^{-1}\) and \(k = \frac{2.303}{4}\log\frac{100}{25} = 0.3466\ \text{h}^{-1}\) — equal.

First order

Concepts to recall

  • First order: \(t_{1/2} = 0.693/k\), independent of \([A]_0\); \(t_{75\%} = 2t_{1/2}\), \(t_{87.5\%} = 3t_{1/2}\).
  • Zero order: \(t_{1/2} \propto [A]_0\).

Q1(c)(iii)d- and f-Block Elements[1]

(iii) Why is \(\ce{KMnO4}\) capable of exhibiting oxidizing properties in neutral, alkaline as well as acidic solutions?

Answer

In \(\ce{KMnO4}\) manganese is in its highest oxidation state, +7, so it can only gain electrons; it is reduced in every medium, to a product that depends on the medium:

\[\text{acidic:}\quad \ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}\] \[\text{neutral / faintly alkaline:}\quad \ce{MnO4- + 2H2O + 3e- -> MnO2 + 4OH-}\] \[\text{strongly alkaline:}\quad \ce{MnO4- + e- -> MnO4^2-}\]

Concepts to recall

  • Mn(+7) in \(\ce{MnO4-}\): highest state ⇒ strong oxidant.
  • Acid → \(\ce{Mn^2+}\) (5 e⁻); neutral → \(\ce{MnO2}\) (3 e⁻); strong alkali → \(\ce{MnO4^2-}\) (1 e⁻).

Q2(a)Solutions[3]

75.2 gm of phenol (\(\ce{C6H5OH}\)) is dissolved in a solvent of \(K_f = 14\). If the depression in freezing point is 7 K. Find the % of phenol that dimerises.

Answer

[Not printed in the original: the mass of solvent. This is the standard problem with 1 kg of solvent, which is assumed here.]

\(M(\ce{C6H5OH}) = 94\) g mol⁻¹: \(m = \dfrac{75.2/94}{1\ \text{kg}} = 0.8\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}\)

Normal depression: \(\Delta T_f(\text{calc}) = K_f m = 14 \times 0.8 = 11.2\ \text{K}\)

\[i = \frac{\Delta T_f(\text{obs})}{\Delta T_f(\text{calc})} = \frac{7}{11.2} = 0.625\]

Dimerisation \(\ce{2C6H5OH <=> (C6H5OH)2}\): if \(\alpha\) is the fraction dimerised, particles \(= 1 - \alpha + \alpha/2 = 1 - \alpha/2\).

\[1 - \frac{\alpha}{2} = 0.625 \;\Rightarrow\; \alpha = 0.75\]

75% of the phenol dimerises

Concepts to recall

  • \(i = \Delta T(\text{obs})/\Delta T(\text{calc})\).
  • Association \(n\mathrm{A} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{A}_n\): \(i = 1 - \alpha(1 - 1/n)\); for dimers \(i = 1 - \alpha/2\).

Q2(c)Solutions[1]

Common salt or calcium chloride is used to clear snow on the roads. Why?

Answer

Dissolved salt lowers the freezing point of water (depression of freezing point, a colligative property), so the ice/snow melts at temperatures below 0 °C and does not re-freeze. \(\ce{CaCl2}\) (\(i \approx 3\)) gives more particles per mole than NaCl (\(i \approx 2\)) and is more effective.

Concepts to recall

  • \(\Delta T_f = iK_f m\): more particles ⇒ larger depression.

t / min\(V_\infty - V_t\) / mL\(k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{11.43}{V_\infty-V_t}\) / min⁻¹
758.95\(\frac{2.303}{75}\log 1.2771 = 3.26\times10^{-3}\)

1936.30\(\frac{2.303}{193}\log 1.8143 = 3.09\times10^{-3}\)

The values of \(k\) are (nearly) constant, so the reaction is first order (pseudo-first-order: water is in large excess). Equivalently, a plot of \(\log(V_\infty - V_t)\) against \(t\) is a straight line:

  • Pseudo-first-order: \(\ce{H2O}\) in large excess, its concentration is effectively constant.
Q3(b)Chemical Kinetics[2]

Define molecularity of a reaction. Give one difference between the order of a reaction and its molecularity.

Answer

Molecularity is the number of reacting species (atoms, ions or molecules) that must collide simultaneously in an elementary step for the reaction to occur.

OrderMolecularity
Sum of the powers of the concentration terms in the experimentally determined rate law. Number of species colliding in an elementary step — a theoretical concept.
Can be zero or fractional; determined by experiment.Always a small whole number (1, 2, rarely 3); never zero.
Applies to the overall reaction.Applies only to an elementary step (for a complex reaction, to the slowest step).

Concepts to recall

  • Rate = \(k[A]^x[B]^y\): order = \(x + y\) (experimental).
  • Molecularity: elementary steps only; integer ≥ 1.
Q4(b)Electrochemistry[1]

Why is it necessary to use a salt bridge in a galvanic cell?

Answer

A salt bridge (inert electrolyte such as KCl or \(\ce{KNO3}\) in agar–agar):

1. completes the internal circuit by allowing ions to move between the two half-cells, and

2. keeps each solution electrically neutral — anions drift towards the anode compartment (where \(\ce{Zn^2+}\) builds up) and cations towards the cathode compartment (where \(\ce{Cu^2+}\) is removed). Without it charge builds up and the current stops at once. It also minimises the liquid-junction potential.

Concepts to recall

  • Galvanic cell: anode (−) oxidation, cathode (+) reduction; electrons flow anode → cathode in the wire.
  • Salt-bridge electrolyte: ions of similar mobility (\(\ce{K+}\), \(\ce{Cl-}\)/\(\ce{NO3-}\)), inert to both half-cells.
Q4(c)Electrochemistry[2]

A current of 4 amperes is passed through a molten solution for 45 minutes. 2.977 gm of metal is deposited. Calculate the charge carried by the metal cation if its atomic mass is 106.4 gm mol⁻¹.

Q4(d)Electrochemistry[2]

For the cell \(\ce{Zn | Zn^2+ (a = 1) || Cu^2+ (a = 1) | Cu}\)

Given that \(E_{\ce{Zn/Zn^2+}} = 0.761\) V, \(E_{\ce{Cu^2+/Cu}} = 0.339\) v

(i) Write the cell reaction. (ii) Calculate the emf and free energy change at 298k involved in the cell. (F = 96500 C eq⁻¹)

Answer

(i) Anode: \(\ce{Zn -> Zn^2+ + 2e-}\); cathode: \(\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}\)

\[\ce{Zn(s) + Cu^2+(aq) -> Zn^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}\]

Q7(b)Coordination Compounds[2]

Using valence bond approach, predict the shape and magnetic behaviour of \(\ce{[Fe(CN)6]^3-}\)

Answer

Fe: [Ar]3d⁶4s²; in \(\ce{[Fe(CN)6]^3-}\) iron is \(\ce{Fe^3+}\): 3d⁵ (5 unpaired electrons in the free ion).

\(\ce{CN-}\) is a strong-field ligand: it forces the 3d electrons to pair, leaving two 3d orbitals empty. These combine with 4s and three 4p orbitals — d²sp³ (inner-orbital, low-spin) hybridisation — and accept six electron pairs from six \(\ce{CN-}\).

Octahedral; paramagnetic with 1 unpaired electron (μ = √(1×3) = 1.73 BM)

Concepts to recall

  • Strong-field ligands (\(\ce{CN-}\), CO, en) cause pairing → inner-orbital d²sp³ (low spin).
  • Spin-only \(\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\) BM.
  • d²sp³ / sp³d² → octahedral; sp³ → tetrahedral; dsp² → square planar.

(i) Glycerol and 2- propanol. (ii) Glucose and fructose.

Answer

(i) Iodoform test. Warm each with iodine and NaOH: propan-2-ol gives a yellow precipitate of iodoform (it contains CH₃CH(OH)–); glycerol does not.

\[\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 + 4I2 + 6NaOH -> CHI3 v + CH3COONa + 5NaI + 5H2O}\]

(ii) Bromine water test. Glucose (an aldose) decolourises bromine water, being oxidised to gluconic acid; fructose (a ketose) does not.

\[\ce{CH2OH(CHOH)4CHO + Br2 + H2O -> CH2OH(CHOH)4COOH + 2HBr}\]

Concepts to recall

  • Iodoform positive: ethanol, propan-2-ol, ethanal, methyl ketones.
  • Bromine water (mild oxidant) oxidises only the aldehyde group: glucose yes, fructose no.
  • Both glucose and fructose reduce Tollens' and Fehling's (fructose via enolisation in alkali).

Q10(c)Biomolecules[2]

(i) Name the functional group common to both glucose and fructose.
