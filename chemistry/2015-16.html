Q1(a)(iii)Chemical Kinetics[1]

Fill in the blanks with appropriate words given below: [Number, nature, lime, monohydric, unity, trihydric, decreases, does not change, dihydric, zero, sweets, four, primary amine, methyl cyanide, nitric acid, nitrous acid, solutions, slow, overall, order, molecularity]

iii) ________ of a reaction is sum of number of molecules of reactant taking part in ________ step of the reaction.

Answer

Molecularity of a reaction is the sum of the number of molecules of reactants taking part in the slow (rate-determining) step of the reaction.

Molecularity; slow

For an elementary reaction molecularity is the number of reacting species in that single step; for a complex reaction it is defined for the slowest step, which governs the rate.

Concepts to recall

  • Molecularity: theoretical, always a whole number (1, 2, rarely 3), never zero.
  • Order: experimental, sum of powers in the rate law; may be zero or fractional.

Q1(c)(iv)Chemical Kinetics[1]

Another [sic: Answer] the following in brief:

(iv) What are the conditions for an effective collision?

Answer

A collision is effective (leads to reaction) only if

  1. the colliding molecules possess energy equal to or greater than the threshold energy (i.e. kinetic energy at least equal to the activation energy), and
  2. they collide with the proper orientation, so that the bonds to be broken and formed are suitably placed.

Concepts to recall

  • Rate = \(Z_{AB}\,e^{-E_a/RT}\times P\) (collision frequency × energy factor × orientation/steric factor) — qualitative idea only.

Q2(a)(i)Electrochemistry[2]

9.65C of electicity is required to deposit all the aluminium from 100 ml of solution of aluminium nitrate. What is the normality of the solution?

Answer

By Faraday's laws, 1 F (96500 C) deposits 1 gram-equivalent of any substance.

\[\text{g-equivalents of Al}^{3+} = \frac{Q}{F} = \frac{9.65}{96500} = 1.0\times10^{-4}\]

These are present in 100 mL = 0.100 L of solution:

\[N = \frac{\text{g-equivalents}}{\text{volume in L}} = \frac{1.0\times10^{-4}}{0.100} = 1.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{N}\]

Normality = 1.0 × 10−3 N (molarity = N/3 = 3.33 × 10−4 M)

As in 1(d)(i), aluminium is not really deposited from aqueous solution; the data are meant for Faraday's law.

Concepts to recall

  • Faraday's 1st law \(m = ZQ\); 1 F = 96500 C deposits 1 g-equivalent.
  • Normality = g-equivalents per litre; for \(\ce{Al^3+}\), N = 3 × M.
Q2(a)(ii)Practical: experiments related to pH change[1]

1M acetic acid has higher PH than 1M nitric acid. Explain.

Answer

Nitric acid is a strong acid and is almost completely ionised, so in 1 M \(\ce{HNO3}\), [H+] ≈ 1 M (pH ≈ 0). Acetic acid is a weak acid, only slightly ionised (\(\ce{CH3COOH <=> CH3COO- + H+}\), about 0.4% at 1 M), so [H+] is far smaller and its pH is higher (≈ 2.4).

Lower [H+] from the weak, partly ionised acid ⇒ higher pH

Concepts to recall

  • pH = −log[H+]: the lower [H+], the higher the pH.
  • 2027 practical: compare the pH of strong and weak acids of the same concentration with pH paper / universal indicator.

A current of 4 amp is passed through a molten solution for 45 mins. 2.977 gm of metal is deposited. Calculate the charge carried by the metal cation if its atomic mass is 106.4 gm.

Q2(c)Chemical Kinetics[2]

State and explain the law of mass action.

Answer

Law of mass action (Guldberg and Waage): at constant temperature, the rate of a chemical reaction is directly proportional to the product of the active masses of the reacting substances, each raised to a power equal to its stoichiometric coefficient in the balanced equation.

Active mass = molar concentration, mol L−1 (shown by [ ]); for a pure solid or liquid it is constant and taken as unity.

For \(\ce{aA + bB -> products}\): \[\text{rate} \propto [A]^a[B]^b \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{rate} = k[A]^a[B]^b\]

where k is the rate constant (rate when every concentration is 1 mol L−1).

Example: \(\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) -> 2HI(g)}\), rate = k[H2][I2].

It holds exactly for an elementary step; for a complex reaction the actual rate law (order) must be found experimentally.

Concepts to recall

  • Active mass = molar concentration.
  • Rate law: rate = k[A]m[B]n; order = m + n (experimental).
Q3(b)Solutions[3]

A mixture contains 0.550 gm of camphor and 0.045 gm of an organic solute freezes at 157°C. The solute contains 93.46% of C and 6.54% of H by weight. What is the molecular formula of the compound?

(F.P of camphor = 178.4°C Kf = 37.70)

Answer

Molar mass from the depression of freezing point

\(\Delta T_f = 178.4 - 157.0 = 21.4\ \text{K}\)

\[M_2 = \frac{1000\,K_f\,w_2}{w_1\,\Delta T_f} = \frac{1000\times37.70\times0.045}{0.550\times21.4} = 144\ \text{g mol}^{-1}\]

Empirical formula

Element%% ÷ at. massratio
C93.4693.46/12 = 7.7887.788/6.488 = 1.2 → 6
H6.546.54/1.008 = 6.4881 → 5

Empirical formula \(\ce{C6H5}\), empirical formula mass = 72 + 5 = 77.

\[n = \frac{M}{77} = \frac{144}{77} = 1.87 \approx 2\]

Q3(c)Solutions[3]

What are azeotropic mixtures? Explain their types with examples.

Answer

Azeotropes are liquid mixtures which boil at a constant temperature and distil without any change in composition (the vapour has the same composition as the liquid). They cannot be separated by fractional distillation.

1. Minimum-boiling azeotropes — formed by solutions showing a large positive deviation from Raoult's law (A–B attractions weaker than A–A and B–B). The total vapour pressure passes through a maximum, so the boiling point passes through a minimum; the azeotrope boils below either component. Example: ethanol (95.6% by mass) + water, b.p. 351 K.

2. Maximum-boiling azeotropes — formed by solutions showing a large negative deviation (A–B attractions stronger). The vapour pressure passes through a minimum and the boiling point through a maximum; the azeotrope boils above either component. Examples: nitric acid (68%) + water, b.p. 393.5 K; HCl (20.2%) + water, b.p. 383 K.

Concepts to recall

  • Ideal solution: obeys Raoult's law, \(\Delta H_{mix} = 0\), \(\Delta V_{mix} = 0\).
  • +ve deviation → max. vapour pressure → min. b.p. azeotrope; −ve deviation → min. vapour pressure → max. b.p. azeotrope.
Q3(d)Practical: qualitative analysis (oxalate)[2]

A small quantity of sparingly soluble calcium oxalate is suspended in pure water dissolves on adding dilute hydrochloric acid to it. Explain why?

Answer

The little calcium oxalate that dissolves is in equilibrium with its ions:

\(\ce{CaC2O4(s) <=> Ca^2+(aq) + C2O4^2-(aq)}\)

Oxalic acid is a weak acid, so the \(\ce{H+}\) ions of HCl combine with the oxalate ions to form feebly ionised oxalic acid:

\(\ce{C2O4^2- + 2H+ -> H2C2O4}\)

This removes \(\ce{C2O4^2-}\) from the solution, so the ionic product [\(\ce{Ca^2+}\)][\(\ce{C2O4^2-}\)] falls below the solubility product. The equilibrium shifts to the right and more solid dissolves until it has all gone:

\(\ce{CaC2O4 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2C2O4}\)

(This is the observation used in salt analysis: the white precipitate of calcium oxalate from the oxalate test is insoluble in acetic acid, which supplies too few \(\ce{H+}\), but dissolves in dilute HCl or \(\ce{HNO3}\).)

Concepts to recall

  • A salt of a weak acid dissolves in a strong acid: the anion is removed as the weak acid.
  • Precipitation needs ionic product > \(K_{sp}\); dissolution when it falls below.
  • Oxalate test: \(\ce{CaCl2}\) → white \(\ce{CaC2O4}\), insoluble in \(\ce{CH3COOH}\), soluble in dil. HCl.
Q4(b)Electrochemistry[3]

Determine the emf of the galvanic cell.

Ag | AgNO3 (0.001M) || AgNO3 (0.1M) | Ag. In what direction will electrons travel in the external circuit when the cell operates, E°Ag+|Ag = 0.80V

Answer

This is a concentration cell; both electrodes are \(\ce{Ag+ | Ag}\).

Electrode potentials (Nernst, 298 K, n = 1):

\(E_{\text{right}} = 0.80 + 0.0591\log(0.1) = 0.80 - 0.0591 = 0.7409\ \text{V}\)

\(E_{\text{left}} = 0.80 + 0.0591\log(0.001) = 0.80 - 0.1773 = 0.6227\ \text{V}\)

\[E_{\text{cell}} = E_{\text{right}} - E_{\text{left}} = 0.0591\log\frac{0.1}{0.001} = 0.0591\times2 = 0.118\ \text{V}\]

Ecell = 0.118 V

Since \(E_{\text{cell}} > 0\), the cell works as written: the left electrode (in 0.001 M) is the anode, \(\ce{Ag -> Ag+ + e-}\); the right electrode (in 0.1 M) is the cathode, \(\ce{Ag+ + e- -> Ag}\).

Electrons flow in the external circuit from the Ag electrode in 0.001 M AgNO3 to the Ag electrode in 0.1 M AgNO3 (left → right)

Concepts to recall

  • Nernst: \(E = E^\circ - \dfrac{0.0591}{n}\log\dfrac{1}{[\ce{M^{n+}}]}\) for \(\ce{M^{n+} + ne- -> M}\).
  • Concentration cell: \(E^\circ_{cell} = 0\); \(E_{cell} = \dfrac{0.0591}{n}\log\dfrac{c_{\text{cathode}}}{c_{\text{anode}}}\).
  • Electrons leave the anode (oxidation, −) through the wire.

Q4(d)(ii)Electrochemistry[2]

Define specific conductance of an electrolytic solution. What is the relationship with molar conductance?

Answer

Specific conductance (conductivity), κ, is the conductance of a solution of unit length and unit area of cross-section, i.e. of 1 cm3 of solution placed between two electrodes 1 cm2 in area and 1 cm apart. It is the reciprocal of resistivity:

\[\kappa = \frac{1}{\rho} = \frac{1}{R}\cdot\frac{l}{A}\qquad\text{unit: S cm}^{-1}\ (\Omega^{-1}\text{cm}^{-1})\]

Molar conductance, \(\Lambda_m\), is the conductance of the volume of solution containing 1 mole of electrolyte. Relation:

\[\Lambda_m = \kappa\times V = \frac{\kappa\times1000}{M}\]

with \(\kappa\) in S cm−1, M the molarity (mol L−1) and \(\Lambda_m\) in S cm2 mol−1.

Concepts to recall

  • V = volume (cm3) containing 1 mol = 1000/M.
  • On dilution \(\kappa\) decreases but \(\Lambda_m\) increases.
Q4(d)(iii)Electrochemistry[1]

AgNO3 solution cannot be stored in a copper vessel. Explain why?

Answer

Copper lies above silver in the electrochemical series (\(E^\circ_{\ce{Cu^2+/Cu}} = +0.34\) V < \(E^\circ_{\ce{Ag+/Ag}} = +0.80\) V), so copper is the stronger reducing agent and displaces silver from solution:

\(\ce{Cu(s) + 2Ag+(aq) -> Cu^2+(aq) + 2Ag(s)}\); \(E^\circ_{cell} = 0.80 - 0.34 = +0.46\) V > 0 (spontaneous).

The vessel would dissolve (corrode) and the solution would turn blue.

Concepts to recall

  • A metal with lower (more negative) \(E^\circ\) displaces a metal of higher \(E^\circ\) from its salt solution.
  • \(E^\circ_{cell} > 0\) ⇒ \(\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ < 0\), spontaneous.

Explain the geometry, magnetic nature and hybridization of central metal atom in [Fe(CN)6]3− using VBT

Answer

Oxidation state of Fe: \(x + 6(-1) = -3 \Rightarrow x = +3\); \(\ce{Fe^3+}\) is 3d5.

\(\ce{CN-}\) is a strong-field ligand, so the 3d electrons are forced to pair, leaving two 3d orbitals empty. These combine with the 4s and three 4p orbitals:

d2sp3 (inner-orbital, low-spin); octahedral; one unpaired electron ⇒ paramagnetic (μ ≈ 1.73 BM)

Concepts to recall

  • d2sp3 / sp3d2 → octahedral; dsp2 → square planar; sp3 → tetrahedral.
  • Spin-only \(\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\) BM.

(ii) Actinides: +3 is common, but they show a wider range, +3 to +7 (e.g. U +6, Np and Pu up to +7), because the 5f, 6d and 7s levels are close in energy.

  • Lanthanoids: 4f series, mainly +3. Actinoids: 5f series, variable states.
Q6(b)d- and f-Block Elements[2]

In a given transition series, there is no significant change in the atomic radii of elements with increase in atomic number. Explain.

Answer

  • Along a transition series the nuclear charge increases by one unit at each step, and the extra electron enters the inner (n−1)d subshell, not the outer ns shell.
  • The d electrons shield the outer ns electrons from the increasing nuclear charge. The increase in nuclear attraction is therefore nearly cancelled by the increased screening, and the effective nuclear charge felt by the outer electrons changes very little.
  • Hence the atomic radii stay almost constant in the middle of the series (after a small initial decrease); towards the end they rise slightly because of repulsion between the paired d electrons.

Concepts to recall

  • Effective nuclear charge = Z − screening.
  • Electron enters (n−1)d: poor change in Zeff for ns electrons.

Q9(c)(i)Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers[2]

Explain the esterification reaction with mechanism.

Answer

Esterification: a carboxylic acid reacts with an alcohol in the presence of a little concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) to give an ester and water; the reaction is reversible.

\(\ce{CH3COOH + C2H5OH <=>[conc. H2SO4] CH3COOC2H5 + H2O}\)

Mechanism (acid-catalysed nucleophilic acyl substitution):

  1. \(\ce{H+}\) protonates the carbonyl oxygen, making the carbonyl carbon more electrophilic.
  2. The lone pair of the alcohol oxygen attacks this carbon, giving a tetrahedral intermediate.
  3. A proton is transferred to one of the –OH groups, turning it into \(\ce{-OH2+}\), a good leaving group.
  4. Water is lost and the C=O is re-formed.
  5. Loss of \(\ce{H+}\) gives the ester and regenerates the catalyst.

Concepts to recall

  • Conc. \(\ce{H2SO4}\) acts as catalyst and dehydrating agent (shifts the equilibrium right).
  • Acyl–oxygen fission: the OH of water comes from the acid.

As a weak acid it forms a salt with a metal oxide (or with sodium):

\(\ce{2CH3CONH2 + HgO -> (CH3CONH)2Hg + H2O}\) (mercury acetamide)

Q10(b)Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids[4]

Account for the following giving reasons:

(i) Acetic acid can be halogenated in the presence of red phosphorous and chlorine but formic acid cannot be halogenated in the same way.

(ii) Tertiary amines do not undergo acetylation.

(iii) The boiling points of amides are higher than those of corresponding acids.

(iv) Unlike phenols, alcohols are easily protonated.

Answer

(i) The Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky reaction replaces an α-hydrogen. Acetic acid has three α-H atoms: \(\ce{CH3COOH + Cl2 ->[red P] ClCH2COOH + HCl}\). Formic acid, HCOOH, has no α-carbon and so no α-hydrogen.

(ii) Acetylation replaces a hydrogen on nitrogen by \(\ce{CH3CO\bond{-}}\) (\(\ce{RNH2 + CH3COCl -> RNHCOCH3 + HCl}\)). A tertiary amine \(\ce{R3N}\) has no N–H, so it cannot be acetylated.

(iii) Amides have two N–H bonds and a highly polar C=O (resonance \(\ce{R-C(=O)-NH2 <-> R-C(-O^-)=NH2+}\)), so they form a more extensive network of intermolecular hydrogen bonds than acids, which mostly exist as dimers. More energy is needed to separate the molecules (acetamide b.p. 494 K, acetic acid 391 K).

(iv) In an alcohol the lone pairs on oxygen are localised (and the alkyl group's +I effect increases their availability), so the oxygen readily accepts \(\ce{H+}\): \(\ce{ROH + H+ -> ROH2+}\). In phenol a lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring by resonance; it is less available, and protonation would destroy this resonance stabilisation.

Concepts to recall

  • HVZ needs α-H; product is the α-halo acid.
  • Acylation needs N–H (1° and 2° amines only).
  • H-bonding raises b.p.; resonance delocalisation lowers basicity.
