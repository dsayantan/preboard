Q1Titrations (KMnO₄ / ammonium iron(II) sulphate)[7]

You are provided with two solutions as follows:

  • C-10 is a solution containing 1.05 gms of potassium manganate (VII), \(\ce{KMnO4}\) per litre.
  • C-11 is a solution prepared by dissolving 14.5 gms of hydrated ammonium iron (II) sulphate crystals, \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4.FeSO4.6H2O}\) per litre.

PROCEDURE: Rinse and fill the burette with the given solution C-10 (\(\ce{KMnO4}\)). Pipette out 20 ml or 25 ml of C-11 (hydrated ammonium iron (II) sulphate solution) and transfer into a clean conical flask. To this, add 20 ml of C-12 (dilute sulphuric acid) specially provided for titration.

Titrate the solution in the conical flask with C-10 (\(\ce{KMnO4}\)) slowly till one drop of this gives a light permanent pink colour to the solution C-11 in the flask. The pink colour should not disappear on shaking the contents in the conical flask.

Repeat the above procedure to get at least two concordant readings.

Tabulate your readings.

State:
(a) The capacity of the pipette used.
(b) The titre value you intend to use in your calculations.

Show the titre value to the Visiting Examiner.

The equations for the above reactions are as follows:

\[\ce{2KMnO4 + 8H2SO4 + 10(NH4)2SO4.FeSO4.6H2O ->}\] \[\ce{K2SO4 + 2MnSO4 + 10(NH4)2SO4 + 5Fe2(SO4)3 + 68H2O}\]

The ionic equation for the reaction is:

\[\ce{2MnO4- + 10Fe^2+ + 16H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 10Fe^3+ + 8H2O}\]

Relative atomic masses: K = 39   Fe = 56   Mn = 55   S = 32   N = 14   H = 1   O = 16

Calculate the following:
(i) The molarity of the solution of potassium manganate(VII) C-10.
(ii) The molarity of hydrated ammonium iron(II) sulphate solution C-11.
(iii) The strength of hydrated ammonium iron(II) sulphate solution C-11.
(iv) The percentage purity of the sample of hydrated ammonium iron (II) sulphate solution.

• Note: Molarity must be calculated up to at least 4 decimal places.

Answer

The burette readings below are sample readings, generated for a sample of about 95% purity and read to the burette least count (0.1 mL graduations, recorded to two decimal places). Use your own titre in the examination; the method of calculation is what to learn.

Pipette used: 20.0 mL of C-11. No indicator is added: \(\ce{KMnO4}\) is self-indicating (colourless → first permanent light pink). The reading is taken at the upper meniscus, since \(\ce{KMnO4}\) is deeply coloured. No warming is needed for \(\ce{Fe^2+}\).

S. No.(A) Initial burette reading (mL)(B) Final burette reading (mL)(B − A) Volume of C-10 (mL)
1 (rough)0.0021.4021.40
20.0021.1021.10
30.5021.6021.10

(a) Capacity of pipette = 20 mL    (b) Titre value (concordant) = 21.10 mL

From the ionic equation, 2 mol \(\ce{MnO4-}\) ≡ 10 mol \(\ce{Fe^2+}\), i.e. 1 mol \(\ce{KMnO4}\) ≡ 5 mol Mohr's salt.

(i) \(M_r(\ce{KMnO4}) = 39 + 55 + 4(16) = 158\) g mol−1

\[M_1 = \frac{1.05\ \text{g L}^{-1}}{158\ \text{g mol}^{-1}} = 0.006646\ \text{M}\]

(ii)

\[\frac{M_1V_1}{M_2V_2} = \frac{1}{5}\;\Rightarrow\;M_2 = \frac{5\,M_1V_1}{V_2} = \frac{5\times0.006646\times21.10}{20} = 0.035058\ \text{M}\]

(iii) \(M_r(\ce{(NH4)2SO4.FeSO4.6H2O}) = 2(18) + 96 + 56 + 96 + 6(18) = 392\) g mol−1

\[\text{strength} = 0.035058\times392 = 13.74\ \text{g L}^{-1}\]

(iv)

\[\%\ \text{purity} = \frac{13.74}{14.5}\times100 = 94.8\%\]

(i) 0.006646 M; (ii) 0.035058 M; (iii) 13.74 g L−1; (iv) 94.8% (with the sample titre 21.10 mL)

With your own titre V mL (20 mL pipette): \(M_2 = 0.0016615\,V\), strength \(= 0.6513\,V\) g L−1, purity \(= 4.492\,V\)%. A titre above 22.26 mL would mean more than 100% "purity" — a sign of an over-shot end point.

Concepts to recall

  • \(\ce{MnO4-}\) gains 5e−, each \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) loses 1e−: mole ratio \(\ce{KMnO4}\) : Mohr's salt = 1 : 5.
  • \(\dfrac{M_1V_1}{n_1} = \dfrac{M_2V_2}{n_2}\) with \(n\) the coefficients; concentration = M × Mr.
  • Dilute \(\ce{H2SO4}\) (not HCl) supplies \(\ce{H+}\) and stops hydrolysis of \(\ce{Fe^2+}\).
  • Mohr's salt Mr = 392; \(\ce{KMnO4}\) = 158. Concordant = two identical titres; no averaging.
Q2(a)Identification of organic compounds (amino group)[2]

[Question 2 carries 4 marks for (a) and (b) together; shown here as 2 each.] You are provided with two organic compounds C-13 and C-14.

Perform the experiments given below on each of the two compounds. Record the changes taking place at every step of the experiment.

Note the smell of the substance formed, if significant, the colour of the solution obtained, the colour of the precipitate produced and any other observations you may have. State the identity of each compound on the basis of the experiments and the observational changes.

PROCEDURE:

(a) Substance C-13

(i) Take 1ml of C-13 in a test tube and add 1ml of concentrated hydrochloric acid to it. Now add a few drops of neutral ferric chloride solution and dilute the contents with water.

(ii) Take 1ml of C-13 in a test tube and add 5ml of bromine water. Shake the contents of the test tube.

(iii) Take 1ml of C-13 in a dry test tube and add a pinch of solid sodium nitrite to it followed by 3-4 drops of concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\). Shake the contents of the test tube. Note the colour. Add 3-4 drops of water to this followed by aqueous sodium hydroxide solution till it becomes alkaline.

Answer

C-13 is a colourless/pale brown oily liquid, sparingly soluble in water.

C-13 is aniline (aminobenzene), \(\ce{C6H5NH2}\) — amino group

Tests (ii) and (iii) are, word for word, steps of the school laboratory manual's aniline experiment, which records a brilliant red dye in (iii); test (i) is the aniline test set in ISC 2019 Paper 2. Phenol also gives a white precipitate with bromine water, so it is (i) (no violet colour) and (iii) (red dye in alkali, not blue/green) that rule phenol out. (The carbylamine test is not performed in the ISC practical.)

Concepts to recall

  • Aniline: oily, sparingly soluble in water, dissolves in HCl (anilinium chloride).
  • Bromine water: white ppt (2,4,6-tribromoaniline) — phenol gives a white ppt too, so pair it with a test that separates them (\(\ce{FeCl3}\): violet for phenol).
  • Oxidising agents (\(\ce{FeCl3}\)/HCl, \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\)/dil. \(\ce{H2SO4}\), bleaching powder) give green / violet / black colours with aniline.
Q2(b)Characteristic tests of carbohydrates (glucose)[2]

(b) Substance C-14

(i) Take 2ml of C-14 solution and add 2ml of Fehling's solution and heat strongly.

(ii) Take 2ml of C-14 solution and add 2ml of Tollen's reagent and heat strongly.

(iii) Take 2ml of C-14 solution and add few drops of caustic soda (NaOH) solution and heat to boil.

Answer

C-14 is a colourless, odourless aqueous solution (of a white crystalline solid).

ExperimentObservationInference
(i) C-14 + Fehling's solution (A + B), heat stronglyDeep blue colour discharged; brick-red precipitateReducing compound (free –CHO group): reducing sugar or aldehyde
(ii) C-14 + Tollens' reagent, heatSilver mirror on the inner wall of the test tube (or a grey-black precipitate)Reducing compound — confirms the –CHO group
(iii) C-14 + a few drops of NaOH, boilYellow colour, deepening to brown (caramel-like smell)Reducing sugar (glucose decomposes/resinifies in hot alkali); formaldehyde would stay colourless
\[\ce{C6H12O6 + 2Cu^2+ + 5OH- -> C6H11O7^- + Cu2O v + 3H2O}\] \[\ce{C6H12O6 + 2[Ag(NH3)2]+ + 3OH- -> C6H11O7^- + 2Ag v + 4NH3 + 2H2O}\]

(\(\ce{C6H11O7-}\) = gluconate ion: the –CHO of glucose is oxidised to –COO−.)

C-14 is a carbohydrate — a reducing sugar, glucose \(\ce{C6H12O6}\)

The three tests are, word for word, the school laboratory manual's glucose experiment ("heat strongly", "C-14 solution"), so glucose is the intended compound. Fehling's and Tollens' alone cannot separate glucose from formaldehyde; test (iii) does: glucose turns yellow then brown with hot alkali, whereas formaldehyde (no \(\alpha\)-H, so no aldol resin) gives no colour and has a sharp pungent smell. If your sample is pungent and stays colourless in (iii), report formaldehyde (aldehyde group) instead. ISC asks for the identification as "carbohydrate" — name the class, then the sugar.

Concepts to recall

  • Fehling's (brick-red \(\ce{Cu2O}\)) and Tollens' (silver mirror): free –CHO — reducing sugars and aldehydes; sucrose and ketones (except fructose, in alkali) do not.
  • Glucose + hot NaOH: yellow → brown; HCHO: no colour (no \(\alpha\)-H).
  • Carbohydrate confirmations: Molisch (violet ring with \(\alpha\)-naphthol / conc. \(\ce{H2SO4}\)).

Concepts to recall

  • Order of anion tests: dil. \(\ce{H2SO4}\) (\(\ce{CO3^2-}\), \(\ce{NO2-}\), \(\ce{S^2-}\), \(\ce{SO3^2-}\)) → conc. \(\ce{H2SO4}\) (\(\ce{Cl-}\), \(\ce{Br-}\), \(\ce{I-}\), \(\ce{NO3-}\), \(\ce{CH3COO-}\)) → special (\(\ce{SO4^2-}\), \(\ce{PO4^3-}\), \(\ce{C2O4^2-}\)).
  • Wet tests use the sodium carbonate extract.
  • Group reagents: 0 NaOH/heat; I dil. HCl; II \(\ce{H2S}\)/dil. HCl; III \(\ce{NH4Cl + NH4OH}\); IV \(\ce{H2S}\)/ \(\ce{NH4OH}\); V \(\ce{(NH4)2CO3}\); VI \(\ce{Na2HPO4}\).
  • \(\ce{Pb^2+}\): \(\ce{PbCl2}\) soluble in hot water; yellow \(\ce{PbCrO4}\), \(\ce{PbI2}\).
