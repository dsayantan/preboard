Q1(a)(iii)Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers[1]

Fill in the blanks by choosing the appropriate word/words from those given in the brackets: (high, electron pair donor, low, proton donor, glycerol, glycol, soaps, salt effect, increased, decreased, common ion effect, cation, anion, cathode, anode, proton acceptor, ester)

(iii) Hydrolysis of fats or oils with sodium hydroxide gives ______ and ______.

Answer

Hydrolysis of fats or oils with sodium hydroxide gives soaps and glycerol.

A fat is a triester of glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol) with long-chain fatty acids; alkaline hydrolysis of the ester (saponification) gives the sodium salts of the acids (soap) and glycerol:

\[\ce{(C17H35COO)3C3H5 + 3NaOH -> 3C17H35COONa + C3H5(OH)3}\]

soaps; glycerol

Concepts to recall

  • Fats/oils are glyceryl esters (triglycerides) of fatty acids.
  • Ester + NaOH → sodium carboxylate + alcohol (saponification).
  • Glycerol = propane-1,2,3-triol, a trihydric alcohol.
Q1(a)(v)Biomolecules[1]

(v) In acidic solution, an amino acid exists as a ______ and migrates towards ______ under the influence of an electric field.

Answer

In acidic solution an amino acid exists as a cation and migrates towards the cathode.

The zwitterion takes up \(\ce{H+}\) on its –COO⁻ group, leaving only the positive –NH₃⁺ group:

\[\ce{H3N+-CH2-COO- + H+ -> H3N+-CH2-COOH}\]

cation; cathode

Concepts to recall

  • Amino acids exist as dipolar ions (zwitterions) \(\ce{H3N+-CHR-COO-}\).
  • Below the isoelectric point (acid) → cation → moves to cathode; above it (alkali) → anion → anode.
  • At the isoelectric point there is no net migration.
Q1(b)(i)Electrochemistry[1]

Complete the following statements by selecting the correct alternative from the choices given:

(i) A standard hydrogen electrode has zero electrode potential because

(a) Hydrogen is the lightest element. (b) Hydrogen has only one electron. (c) The electrode potential is assumed to be zero. (d) None of these.

Answer

(c) The electrode potential is assumed to be zero.

A single electrode potential cannot be measured on its own; only differences can. The SHE (Pt, \(\ce{H2}\) at 1 bar, \(\ce{H+}\) at unit activity, 298 K) is chosen as the reference and its potential is assigned the value 0.00 V by convention.

Concepts to recall

  • SHE: Pt | H₂(g, 1 bar) | H⁺(aq, unit activity); E° = 0 by convention.
  • \(E^\circ_\text{cell} = E^\circ_\text{cathode} - E^\circ_\text{anode}\), measured against the SHE.
Q1(b)(ii)Solutions[1]

(ii) For an associated solute in a solution the value of Vant Hoff factor is:

(1) One (2) Two (3) Greater than one (4) Less than one

Answer

(4) Less than one

\(i = \dfrac{\text{observed colligative property}}{\text{normal value}} = \dfrac{\text{number of particles after association}}{\text{number before}}\). Association (e.g. ethanoic acid dimerising in benzene) reduces the number of particles, so \(i < 1\); for complete dimerisation \(i = \tfrac12\).

Concepts to recall

  • Dissociation: \(i = 1 + (n-1)\alpha > 1\).
  • Association into \(n\)-mers: \(i = 1 - \alpha\left(1 - \tfrac1n\right) < 1\).
Q1(b)(iii)Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers[1]

(iii) The major product formed when phenol is heated with carbon tetrachloride in presence of sodium hydroxide followed by acid hydrolysis is

(1) 4-hydroxy benzoic acid (2) Salicylic acid (3) Phenyl chloride (4) Salicylaldehyde

Answer

(2) Salicylic acid

This is the Reimer–Tiemann reaction with \(\ce{CCl4}\) in place of \(\ce{CHCl3}\): the \(\ce{-CCl3}\) group enters mainly ortho to the phenoxide oxygen and is hydrolysed to \(\ce{-COOH}\). (With chloroform the product would be salicylaldehyde.)

\[\ce{C6H5OH + CCl4 + 6NaOH -> C6H4(ONa)COONa + 4NaCl + 4H2O}\quad(\text{ortho})\] \[\ce{C6H4(ONa)COONa + 2HCl -> C6H4(OH)COOH + 2NaCl}\]

Concepts to recall

  • Reimer–Tiemann: phenol + \(\ce{CHCl3}\)/NaOH → salicylaldehyde (\(\ce{-CHO}\) ortho).
  • With \(\ce{CCl4}\)/NaOH the ortho product is salicylic acid (\(\ce{-COOH}\)).
  • Kolbe's reaction (sodium phenoxide + \(\ce{CO2}\), 400 K, 4–7 atm) also gives salicylic acid.
Q1(b)(v)Solutions[1]

(v) This is not a colligative property:

(1) Lowering of vapour pressure (2) Osmotic pressure (3) Elevation of boiling point (4) Depression of freezing point

Answer

(1) Lowering of vapour pressure

By Raoult's law \(p^\circ - p = p^\circ x_B\): the lowering itself depends on \(p^\circ\), which is a property of the solvent, so it is not purely colligative. It is the relative lowering \(\dfrac{p^\circ-p}{p^\circ} = x_B\) that depends only on the number of solute particles. (2), (3) and (4) are colligative.

Concepts to recall

  • Colligative = depends only on the number of solute particles, not their nature.
  • The four: relative lowering of vapour pressure, \(\Delta T_b\), \(\Delta T_f\), osmotic pressure.
  • Raoult: \((p^\circ - p)/p^\circ = x_\text{solute}\).

  • Carbylamine (isocyanide) test: 1° amine + \(\ce{CHCl3}\) + 3 KOH (alc) → \(\ce{RNC}\) + 3 KCl + 3 \(\ce{H2O}\).

Q1(c)(iii)Solutions[1]

(iii) The osmotic pressure produced by dilute equimolar solutions of three substances are in the order X > glucose > Y. Suggest a reason for this behavior.

Answer

Osmotic pressure \(\pi = iCRT\) depends on the number of solute particles. Glucose neither dissociates nor associates (\(i = 1\)). At equal concentration:

• X gives a larger \(\pi\) → \(i > 1\) → X dissociates (ionises) in solution, e.g. NaCl.

• Y gives a smaller \(\pi\) → \(i < 1\) → Y associates in solution (e.g. dimerises).

X dissociates (i > 1); glucose is normal (i = 1); Y associates (i < 1)

Concepts to recall

  • \(\pi = iCRT\); at the same \(C\) and \(T\), \(\pi \propto i\).
  • Dissociation → \(i > 1\); association → \(i < 1\); non-electrolyte → \(i = 1\).
Q1(c)(iv)Electrochemistry[1]

(iv) Same quantity of electricity is passed separately through aqueous solution of copper(I) sulphate and copper(II) sulphate using inert electrodes. In which case the amount of copper deposited will be more and why?

Answer

More copper is deposited from the copper(I) solution — twice as much.

\[\ce{Cu+ + e- -> Cu}\qquad\] \[\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}\]

By Faraday's second law the mass deposited by the same charge is proportional to the equivalent mass \(M/z\): 1 F deposits 63.5 g of Cu from \(\ce{Cu+}\) (z = 1) but only 31.75 g from \(\ce{Cu^2+}\) (z = 2).

Cu(I) sulphate: mass ratio Cu(I) : Cu(II) = 2 : 1

Concepts to recall

  • Faraday I: \(m = ZQ = \dfrac{M}{zF}\,It\).
  • Faraday II: same charge → masses in the ratio of equivalent masses \(M/z\).

Q2(b)(ii)Electrochemistry[1]

Explain why:

(ii) Specific conductance of an electrolyte decreases with dilution.

Answer

Specific conductance (conductivity) \(\kappa\) is the conductance of 1 cm³ of the solution (between electrodes 1 cm² in area, 1 cm apart). On dilution, although the degree of ionisation rises, the number of ions per unit volume falls (the same ions are spread through more solvent), so \(\kappa\) decreases.

Molar conductivity \(\Lambda_m = \kappa \times 1000/C\) refers to a whole mole of electrolyte, so it increases with dilution.

Concepts to recall

  • \(\kappa = G \times \dfrac{l}{A}\) — conductance of unit volume (S cm⁻¹).
  • \(\Lambda_m = \kappa \times 1000 / C\) (S cm² mol⁻¹).
  • Dilution: ions per cm³ ↓ ⇒ κ ↓; ionisation and ion mobility ↑ ⇒ Λm ↑.
Q2(c)Electrochemistry[2]

The specific conductivity of 0.02 M KCl solution at 25°C is \(2.768\times10^{-3}\) ohm⁻¹ cm⁻¹. The resistance of the solution at 25°C using a particular cell was 250.2 ohm. The resistance of 0.01 M \(\ce{CuSO4}\) solution at 25°C measured with the same cell was 8331 ohms. Calculate the molar conductivity of the copper sulphate solution.

Answer

Cell constant from the KCl standard: \(\kappa = \dfrac{1}{R}\times\dfrac{l}{A}\Rightarrow \dfrac{l}{A} = \kappa R\)

\[\frac{l}{A} = 2.768\times10^{-3}\ \text{S cm}^{-1}\times 250.2\ \Omega = 0.6925\ \text{cm}^{-1}\]

Conductivity of the \(\ce{CuSO4}\) solution (same cell):

\[\kappa = \frac{l/A}{R} = \frac{0.6925}{8331} = 8.313\times10^{-5}\ \text{S cm}^{-1}\]

Molar conductivity:

\[\Lambda_m = \frac{\kappa\times1000}{C} = \frac{8.313\times10^{-5}\times1000}{0.01} = 8.313\ \text{S cm}^2\,\text{mol}^{-1}\]

\(\Lambda_m(\ce{CuSO4}) = 8.31\ \text{S cm}^2\,\text{mol}^{-1}\ (= 8.31\times10^{-4}\ \text{S m}^2\,\text{mol}^{-1})\)

Concepts to recall

  • Cell constant \(G^* = l/A = \kappa R\), found with a KCl solution of known κ.
  • \(\kappa = G^*/R\); \(\Lambda_m = 1000\,\kappa/C\) (κ in S cm⁻¹, C in mol L⁻¹).
Q2(d)Solutions[2]

Benzene and toluene form ideal solution. At 310 K, the vapour pressure of pure benzene is 150 mm Hg and of pure toluene 45 mm Hg. Calculate the vapour pressure of a mixture of these two containing equal masses at 310 K.

Answer

Take 100 g of each. \(M(\ce{C6H6}) = 78\), \(M(\ce{C7H8}) = 92\) g mol⁻¹.

\[n_B = \frac{100}{78} = 1.282\ \text{mol},\qquad n_T = \frac{100}{92} = 1.087\ \text{mol}\] \[x_B = \frac{1.282}{1.282+1.087} = 0.541,\qquad x_T = 1 - 0.541 = 0.459\]

Raoult's law for an ideal solution: \(p = p^\circ_B x_B + p^\circ_T x_T\)

\[p = 150(0.541) + 45(0.459) = 81.2 + 20.6 = 101.8\ \text{mm Hg}\]

Vapour pressure of the mixture ≈ 101.8 mm Hg

Concepts to recall

  • Raoult's law: \(p_i = p_i^\circ x_i\); total \(p = \sum p_i^\circ x_i\) for an ideal solution.
  • Equal masses ≠ equal moles: convert with molar masses first.
Q2(e)Solutions[2]

A solution containing 10.2 g of glycerine per litre is found to be isotonic with a 2% solution of glucose. Calculate the molecular mass of glycerine. (Molecular weight of glucose is 180)

Answer

Isotonic solutions (same T) have equal osmotic pressure, hence equal molar concentration (\(\pi = CRT\), both non-electrolytes).

2% glucose = 2 g per 100 mL = 20 g L⁻¹:

\[C_\text{glucose} = \frac{20}{180} = 0.1111\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\] \[\frac{10.2}{M} = 0.1111 \;\Rightarrow\; M = \frac{10.2}{0.1111} = 91.8\ \text{g mol}^{-1}\]

Molar mass of glycerine ≈ 91.8 g mol⁻¹ (true value 92)

Concepts to recall

  • Isotonic ⇒ equal π ⇒ equal \(iC\) at the same temperature.
  • \(\pi = \dfrac{w}{MV}RT\); a "2 % solution" is 2 g per 100 mL.
Q3(c)Electrochemistry[2]

The molar conductance of NaOH, NaCl and \(\ce{BaCl2}\) at infinite dilutions are \(2.481\times10^{-2}\), \(1.265\times10^{-2}\) and \(2.8\times10^{-2}\) S m² mol⁻¹ respectively; calculate \(\lambda^\infty_m\) for \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\).

Answer

By Kohlrausch's law of independent migration of ions, \(\Lambda^\circ_m\) of an electrolyte is the sum of the limiting molar conductivities of its ions:

\[\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{Ba(OH)2}) = \lambda^\circ_{\ce{Ba^2+}} + 2\lambda^\circ_{\ce{OH-}} = \Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{BaCl2}) + 2\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{NaOH}) - 2\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{NaCl})\] \[= 2.8\times10^{-2} + 2(2.481\times10^{-2}) - 2(1.265\times10^{-2})\] \[= (2.800 + 4.962 - 2.530)\times10^{-2} = 5.232\times10^{-2}\ \text{S m}^2\,\text{mol}^{-1}\]

\(\Lambda^\circ_m(\ce{Ba(OH)2}) = 5.232\times10^{-2}\ \text{S m}^2\,\text{mol}^{-1}\)

Concepts to recall

  • Kohlrausch: \(\Lambda^\circ_m = \nu_+\lambda^\circ_+ + \nu_-\lambda^\circ_-\).
  • Combine known \(\Lambda^\circ_m\) values so that unwanted ions (here \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{Cl-}\)) cancel.
Q3(d)Solutions[3]

A 0.1539 molal aqueous solution of cane sugar (molar mass = 342 g mol⁻¹) has a freezing point of 271 K while the freezing point of pure water is 273.15 K. What will be the freezing point of an aqueous solution containing 5 g of glucose (mol mass = 180 g mol⁻¹) per 100 g of solution?

Answer

\(K_f\) from the cane-sugar data: \(\Delta T_f = K_f\, m\)

\[\Delta T_f = 273.15 - 271 = 2.15\ \text{K};\qquad K_f = \frac{2.15}{0.1539} = 13.97\ \text{K kg mol}^{-1}\]

Molality of the glucose solution: 5 g glucose in 100 g solution ⇒ 95 g (0.095 kg) water.

\[m = \frac{5/180}{0.095} = \frac{0.02778}{0.095} = 0.2924\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}\] \[\Delta T_f = 13.97 \times 0.2924 = 4.08\ \text{K}\] \[T_f = 273.15 - 4.08 = 269.07\ \text{K}\]

Freezing point of the glucose solution ≈ 269.07 K

Note: 0.1539 mol kg⁻¹ is exactly a 5% (w/w) cane-sugar solution (5 g in 95 g water). The \(K_f\) these data imply, 13.97 K kg mol⁻¹, is far larger than the real value for water (1.86); the paper uses textbook data, and the answer is worked with the value the data give, as intended.

Concepts to recall

  • \(\Delta T_f = K_f m\), \(m = \dfrac{w_B/M_B}{W_A\ (\text{kg})}\).
  • "Per 100 g of solution": subtract the solute mass to get the solvent mass.
  • Same solvent ⇒ same \(K_f\): ratio method \(\Delta T_1/\Delta T_2 = m_1/m_2\).
Q4(b)Solutions[2]

A 0.5% aqueous solution of potassium chloride freezes at −0.24°C. Calculate the Van't Hoff factor.

Answer

\(K_f\) for water is not printed; the standard value 1.86 K kg mol⁻¹ is used.

0.5% (w/w): 0.5 g KCl (M = 74.5 g mol⁻¹) in 99.5 g water.

\[m = \frac{0.5/74.5}{0.0995} = 0.06745\ \text{mol kg}^{-1}\]

Normal (calculated) depression: \(\Delta T_f(\text{calc}) = K_f m = 1.86 \times 0.06745 = 0.1255\ \text{K}\)

Observed depression: \(\Delta T_f(\text{obs}) = 0 - (-0.24) = 0.24\ \text{K}\)

\[i = \frac{\Delta T_f(\text{obs})}{\Delta T_f(\text{calc})} = \frac{0.24}{0.1255} = 1.91\]

van't Hoff factor \(i \approx 1.91\) — close to 2, since KCl dissociates almost completely into \(\ce{K+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) (degree of dissociation \(\alpha = i - 1 = 0.91\)).

[Not printed in the original: the value of \(K_f\) for water (taken as 1.86 K kg mol⁻¹).]

Concepts to recall

  • \(i = \dfrac{\Delta T_f(\text{obs})}{\Delta T_f(\text{calc})}\)
  • \(\Delta T_f = iK_f m\); for KCl, \(i = 1 + \alpha\).
  • \(K_f(\ce{H2O}) = 1.86\ \text{K kg mol}^{-1}\).
